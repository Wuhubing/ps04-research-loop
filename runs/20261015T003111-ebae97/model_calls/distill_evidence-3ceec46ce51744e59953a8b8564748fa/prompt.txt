The trainable span is the complete sampled meta-reasoning decision response, including its emitted reasoning and resulting work order or termination decision. Prompts, curated observations, executor generations, tool calls, and experiments are masked.\n5.4 Buffered Single-Rollout Asynchronous Policy Optimization\nInvestigations have heterogeneous wall-clock durations, so synchronized rollout cohorts can leave capacity idle. MIRA-AC instead collects completed episodes continuously into separate actor and critic buffers, which trigger updates independently on the shared model. If both are ready, the critic update is applied first, followed by the actor update within the same atomic update transaction. The resulting parameters are published once. A live episode adopts newly published parameters only at its next research-decision boundary and may therefore span multiple model versions.\nAlgorithm [2](#alg2) summarizes training. Buffer thresholds, optimizer\nsettings, rollout limits, and details of low-rank adaptation\n(LoRA) ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib71)) are given in\nApp. [H](#A8).\n6 Experiments\nWe evaluate the approach in four stages. First, we ask whether MIRA improves long-horizon inference before policy training. Second, we test whether remaining research value can be forecast at MIRA’s decision boundaries and whether this forecasting capability transfers across research environments. Third, we evaluate whether decision-level credit improves the research policy and whether the learned policy transfers across environments. Finally, we analyze how training changes research behavior and how MIRA’s decision-level decomposition affects where policy learning is applied and the cost of collecting long-horizon trajectories.\nThese stages place different demands on the experimental setting. To evaluate MIRA before learning, we use full-scale long-horizon settings in theorem proving and open-ended neural-architecture research. The policy that generates research decisions is trained, while evidence curation and execution remain fixed. This makes learning tractable and provides a clean credit-assignment boundary, but it also means that the policy can act only on evidence surfaced by the curator and can improve outcomes only through investigations the executor is capable of carrying out. Relevant evidence omitted during curation cannot be recovered by the actor or critic, and improvements in the executor may change which research decisions are valuable. An important next step is therefore to learn curation, allocation, and possibly execution jointly while preserving decision boundaries for credit assignment.\nThe behavioral results raise a broader question: what should a general meta-reasoner learn about how to conduct research? Policy training shapes not only task performance, but the model’s research temperament—how readily it abandons an incumbent, gathers more evidence, or commits to a larger change. In HillClimbBench-CPU, MIRA-AC’s willingness to pursue substantial architectural changes can escape local optima that trap untrained MIRA on some tasks, yet the same willingness can backfire when preserving and patiently refining the incumbent is better. The calibration problem recurs elsewhere: a new experiment is useful while physical laws remain ambiguous but wasteful once they are resolved; a richer symbolic expression can improve proxy fit while harming extrapolation; and broad graph search can be inferior to a one-edge repair. The objective is therefore not maximal exploration or aggressiveness, but context-sensitive judgment about when to persist, refine, test, pivot, or stop.\nThe environments used for policy training cover only a small fraction of the diversity required to learn this judgment. A general policy will require a deliberately constructed training distribution spanning distinct research regimes—exploration and exploitation, bold pivots and incremental refinement, continued investigation and stopping—so that the model learns not one research style, but which style and granularity of investigation a situation warrants. In this work, we demonstrate that decision-level training improves outcomes, changes research behavior, and transfers to an environment held out from actor training, providing a roadmap for scaling toward that goal.\nWe demonstrate that meta-reasoning updates transfer to an environment held out from actor training: MIRA-AC improves on BNLearn despite receiving no BNLearn actor updates. The model is, however, exposed to BNLearn during value pretraining. Future work should examine broader transfer to environments excluded from both value pretraining and actor training.\nOur experiments use a single MIRA configuration with the same Codex-based inner-loop harness across research environments. This holds the research interface fixed while varying the task. Training across heterogeneous and evolving inner-loop scaffolds is a natural next step for testing whether learned meta-reasoning remains useful as execution capabilities change.\nOur learning experiments also depend on proxy feedback. Dense proxy signals make repeated autoresearch training feasible, but a proxy need not rank research directions in the same way as the final gold evaluation. Our results show that proxy-trained policies can improve gold-evaluated outcomes in the environments studied here, but they do not establish when such transfer should be expected or how robust learning is to systematically misaligned proxies. Future work should characterize the degree of proxy–gold alignment required for stable policy improvement and develop methods that can learn from sparse gold feedback without giving up the sample efficiency of intermediate signals.\nThe behavioral analysis should likewise be interpreted as descriptive. Its conclusions rely on an LLM judge assigning process labels and comparing matched trajectories. These annotations provide evidence that training changes observable research behavior, but they do not establish that any individual behavior—such as testing competing hypotheses, abandoning stalled directions, or making larger pivots—causes the associated performance difference. More direct interventions on research strategy, together with human or task-specific behavioral evaluation, would be needed to identify which learned behaviors are causally useful and under which research regimes.\nFinally, the current optimization results are limited by scale. We train LoRA adapters rather than all model parameters, and our asynchronous procedure is evaluated only at investigation durations supported by the present infrastructure. We have not tested episodes in which individual investigations last hours or days and a single trajectory spans many more actor and critic versions. At those timescales, delayed and heterogeneous feedback, policy staleness, and interference between actor and critic updates to shared parameters may become substantially more important. Scaling to full-parameter training and longer-lived research episodes will therefore require studying update scheduling, value calibration under changing policies, and stable joint optimization over much larger temporal gaps.\nMore broadly, MIRA-AC assumes that research can be decomposed into meaningful decision boundaries at which accumulated evidence is summarized, future value can be estimated, and credit can be assigned. The environments studied here support that decomposition. Whether the same abstraction remains effective for less structured research, where useful investigations overlap, branch, or evolve continuously, is an open question. Testing that boundary is important for determining how far decision-level meta-reasoning can scale toward more general autonomous research.\n8 Conclusion\nThis work asks whether a research agent can learn what to investigate next from the downstream consequences of its previous research decisions. Across the settings studied here, our results support a positive answer. MIRA represents long-horizon research as a sequence of explicit meta-reasoning decisions over a persistent research record, treating the potentially much longer execution of each investigation as part of an environment transition. This decomposition improves long-horizon inference even before policy training and provides a natural boundary at which remaining research value can be forecast and delayed outcomes assigned to earlier decisions.\nBuilding on this representation, MIRA-AC jointly trains one model to forecast remaining return from a curated research observation and choose how to allocate research effort next. Decision-level value forecasting outperforms token-level alternatives, and decision-level policy learning improves gold-evaluated research outcomes, including when trained from proxy feedback, without directly optimizing the longer executor trajectories. We also find initial evidence that actor updates learned across research environments can transfer to a held-out actor-training environment when initialized from a cross-environment value model.\nThe broader implication is that research strategy need not remain an implicit byproduct of long-horizon generation. Once decisions about what to investigate are represented explicitly and linked to their downstream consequences, they become objects that can be valued, credited, and improved through experience. meta-reasoning.\nMIRA builds on the classical account in Principles of\nMetareasoning ([Russell and Wefald, 1991](#bib.bib57)), which asks which computation a\nresource-bounded agent should perform next and when it should stop. In long-horizon research, the\nrelevant computations are investigations: testing a hypothesis, revising an\nimplementation, gathering evidence, or terminating the search. MIRA makes\nthese allocation decisions explicit as work orders, thereby turning a\nclassical meta-reasoning problem into a trainable sequential decision process.\nBenchmarks for autonomous research.\nRecent benchmarks expose complementary aspects of long-horizon research.\nMLAgentBench ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) and\nMLE-bench ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) evaluate iterative machine-learning\nengineering over executable tasks, while\nRE-Bench ([Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)) compares agents with human experts over\nincreasing time budgets. PaperBench ([Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) evaluates\nend-to-end replication using author-developed hierarchical rubrics, and\nHCAST ([Rein et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) grounds task difficulty in the time required by\nskilled humans. ScienceAgentBench ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)) similarly\nderives data-driven discovery tasks from published scientific workflows.\nOther environments broaden either the training interface or the space of\nresearch problems. MLE-Dojo ([Qiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) provides interactive\nKaggle environments with normalized rewards suitable for collecting learning\ntrajectories; PostTrainBench ([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) extends\nautoresearch to coupled post-training decisions; and\nFrontierScience ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib11)) targets expert-level\nscientific tasks. DiscoGen ([Goldie et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)) supplies procedurally\ngenerated algorithm-discovery tasks with meta-train/meta-test splits, whereas\nAlgoTune ([Press et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)) evaluates measurable speedups to established\nnumerical programs. Finally, Frontier-Eng ([Chi et al., 2026](#bib.bib17))\ncharacterizes how improvements diminish over extended engineering runs, and\nFML-bench ([Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)) compares search strategies across\ncontrolled landscapes. In addition to introducing HillClimbBench, we cast\nseveral existing environments as instances of a common research decision\nprocess and ask whether research strategy can be learned across trajectories.\nScientific and algorithmic discovery agents.\nSeveral systems automate substantial portions of the research lifecycle. The\nAI Scientist ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)) and\nAI Scientist-v2 ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)) combine experiment execution,\nevaluation, and paper generation, with the latter adding tree search and\nstructured memory. FunSearch ([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) established\nlanguage-model-guided evolution as a mechanism for discovering programs, and\nAlphaEvolve ([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)) scales this approach through model\nensembles, evaluation cascades, and quality-diversity search.\nSAGA ([Du et al., 2025](#bib.bib44)) extends automation to the evolution of\nscientific objectives, while the AI co-scientist ([Gottweis et al., 2026](#bib.bib49))\nand AutoDiscovery ([Agarwal et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)) organize hypothesis\ndevelopment through evolutionary selection and Bayesian surprise,\nrespectively.\nResearch agents also differ in how they divide and retain work.\nR&D-Agent ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib37)) separates researcher and developer roles,\nAutoScientists ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)) coordinates decentralized teams\nthrough shared experimental memory, and MARS ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) performs\nbudget-aware tree search over repository states.\nAutoSOTA ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)) targets reproduction and improvement of\nexisting models, LLM-AutoSciLab ([Kabra et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)) casts\ndiscovery as active experimental design, and Arbor ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib52))\nrefines explicit hypothesis trees.\nDiscoverPhysics ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)) evaluates open-ended\nscientific reasoning in physics, whereas AInstein ([Mishra et al., 2025](#bib.bib50))\nstudies iterative critique using parametric knowledge alone. These systems\nprimarily improve a fixed model through inference-time organization. MIRA is\ncompatible with such execution mechanisms, but our central concern is learning\nthe policy that chooses the next research objective from prior experience.\nSearch, memory, and evolving agent structure.\nExisting agents encode a wide range of search biases.\nAIDE ([Jiang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib38)) uses explicit drafting, improvement, and debugging\noperators; LATS ([Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)) combines tree search with acting,\nplanning, reflection, and model-generated values; and\nLoongFlow ([Wan et al., 2025a](#bib.bib33)) couples plan–execute–summarize cycles\nwith evolutionary archives. DataMaster ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)) schedules\nexploration over the available budget, AIRA ([Pepe et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)) applies\nUCT-style search to neural-architecture discovery, and\nSciMaster ([Chai et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)) distributes exploration across multiple\nagents. Automated AI-research Speedrunning ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) provides\na controlled autoresearch setting, while Agent\nLaboratory ([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)) organizes research into fixed phases. The\ndiversity of successful scaffolds, together with the strategy-dependent results\nof Frontier-Eng ([Chi et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)) and\nFML-bench ([Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), motivates selecting research actions\nconditionally rather than committing to one search heuristic for an entire\ninvestigation.\nA complementary line of work changes the agent’s persistent artifacts.\nSkillGrad ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib20)) treats trajectory diagnoses as textual\ngradients, and SkillOpt ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)) uses bounded edits and\nvalidation to improve reusable skill documents. Harness self-evolution\nanalysis ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)) distinguishes the ability to modify a harness\nfrom the ability to benefit from those modifications, while Compiling Agentic\nWorkflows into LLM Weights ([Dennis et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) shows that procedural\nworkflows can be distilled into model weights. MIRA keeps the execution harness fixed in our\nlearning experiments and instead updates the model that allocates research\neffort; evolving the harness and learning the meta-reasoning policy are\ntherefore complementary directions.\nFeedback and value learning.\nLanguage feedback can convey substantially more structure than a scalar\nreward, as illustrated empirically by LLF-Bench ([Cheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib22))\nand formalized by Formalizing Learning from Language Feedback with Provable\nGuarantees ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). Training AI Co-Scientists Using Rubric\nRewards ([Goel et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)) uses rubric rewards to train research-plan\ngeneration, while HypoAgents ([Duan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)) uses Bayesian\nbelief and entropy updates to guide which hypotheses to refine. Our experiments instead use\nscalar, outcome-grounded targets: either a gold-binary outcome or changes in\na proxy experimental metric. This choice isolates whether delayed research\noutcomes alone can train value estimates at MIRA’s decision boundaries;\nversioned artifacts could support richer feedback in future work.\nMIRA-AC also relates to shared actor–critic representation learning.\nA3C ([Mnih et al., 2016](#bib.bib58)) uses a shared backbone with separate policy and\nscalar-value outputs, while Phasic Policy Gradient ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib59))\nstudies the balance between useful representation transfer and interference\nbetween policy and value objectives. UNREAL ([Jaderberg et al., 2017](#bib.bib60))\ndemonstrates how auxiliary prediction objectives can shape a shared policy\nrepresentation. More recent generative approaches express verification or\nvalue estimation through language modeling itself: Generative\nVerifiers ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)) formulates verification as\nnext-token prediction, and GenAC ([Shan et al., 2026](#bib.bib62)) uses a generative\ncritic for value modeling. MIRA-AC similarly uses\none language model and one next-token interface for both research actions and\nreturn prediction, without adding a scalar head or maintaining a separately\nresident critic.\nCredit assignment in multi-turn agents.\nSeveral methods localize outcome supervision within long interactions. Credit\nassignment can also proceed through a learned step-level critic: Iterative\nGRPO ([Jiang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib4)) fits a turn-level \\(Q^{\\pi}\\) from logged Monte Carlo returns and\nuses it as the reward model for single-turn policy improvement in batch-online\nmulti-turn settings.\nCredit Assignment with Resets ([Samanta et al., 2026a](#bib.bib56)) uses counterfactual\ncontinuations from intermediate states, and Tree-GRPO ([Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib24))\nobtains step-level relative signals from sibling branches that share a prefix.\nR1-Searcher ([Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)) learns retrieval behavior from\noutcome-only reinforcement learning, StepSearch ([Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib29))\napplies step-wise policy optimization to search-augmented reasoning, and\nstrategy-guided exploration ([Szot et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) operates over\nhigh-level language strategies. PokeeResearch ([Wan et al., 2025b](#bib.bib46))\nstudies reinforcement-learning design choices for deep-research agents.\nMIRA-AC differs in where it places the\nlearning boundary: it reconstructs complete research episodes but applies\npolicy loss only to the complete sampled decision responses that allocate future\ncomputation. Its decision-level generative critic assigns delayed credit at those\nboundaries, and buffered asynchronous optimization accommodates investigations\nwith heterogeneous durations.\nAppendix B Experimental Setup\nWe study four autoresearch environments: BNLearn causal discovery, Physics Discovery, SRBench symbolic regression, and HillClimbBench-CPU architecture research. In the reported policy evaluation, BNLearn, Physics, and SRBench each contain 100 tasks with one rollout per task. HillClimbBench-CPU contains 50 distinct paper-derived tasks with two independently initialized rollouts per task. Each rollout permits at most 20 MIRA iterations.\nB.1 Shared Interaction Protocol\nEach task is an isolated, persistent workspace. At every iteration, a fresh\nexecutor receives the MIRA work order, the fixed task prompt, and stable\nenvironment-specific instructions. The prompt identifies the available data\nand tools, interaction budget, editable files, submission artifact, and hidden\ninformation. The executor may write analyses and programs, run proxy\nevaluators, update its artifact, and leave evidence for later iterations. The\nshared procedure governing outer-loop and executor interactions is described in\nApp. [C](#A3); below we specify the environment-facing\npart of the prompt.\nFor the three scientific environments, policy training and reported evaluation use independently initialized trajectories on the same fixed task identities and seeds. They therefore test new research trajectories and held-out evaluations within the same tasks, not generalization to unseen task identities.\nB.2 Defining Proxy Hill-Climbing Signals\nEach environment exposes a different proxy measure of progress: graph-edit distance, trajectory error, expression error, or architecture performance. We map that measure to a higher-is-better quality \\(q_{t}\\in[0,1]\\) after iteration \\(t\\), with \\(q_{0}\\) denoting the fixed initial quality. We construct each map from constants in the environment specification and freeze it before training.\nThe four maps use natural task-level anchors. BNLearn measures how much of the empty graph’s structural Hamming distance (SHD) has been removed. Physics scales trajectory mean squared error (MSE) by position variance and applies a fixed reciprocal map capped at \\(0.95\\). SRBench measures log-normalized mean squared error (log-NMSE) improvement from the initial expression toward the float64 numerical floor. HillClimbBench-CPU measures improvement from the supplied reference architecture toward the task’s ideal score of one. The exact equations appear below. The endpoints need not be attainable; they are placed to distinguish plausible within-budget improvements.\nThese proxies provide a task-local ordering of partial progress. They turn changes across successive research iterations into marginal rewards at a resolution suited to the current model class and fixed research budget. The mapping remains imperfect: it can miss real progress, reward a brittle shortcut, or saturate too early. Gold evaluation therefore remains the final measure of the selected artifact. It stays hidden during research, while reward depends on increases in best-so-far proxy quality.\nFor a new environment, the construction begins with a repeatable proxy metric that usually moves with the desired outcome. Natural anchors include a supplied baseline, an empty prediction, or a task-defined target. A monotone transform can spread raw errors that span orders of magnitude, while its scale should keep plausible improvements away from a narrow or saturated range. Invalid artifacts receive no improvement. The completed map is frozen before policy training. Development tasks then establish its usable range and its association with gold outcomes; poor alignment calls for redesign before final evaluation.\nThe \\(q_{t}\\) values define best-so-far progress and marginal reward in\nSec. [2.2](#S2.SS2); other visible diagnostics do not affect reward. After\nselection, a hidden gold evaluation produces the score reported in the results\ntable. It uses the same hidden target as the proxy for BNLearn and separate\nheld-out data or conditions for the other environments. A task is solved when\nthis gold evaluation meets the environment-specific success criterion.\nB.3 Environment Interfaces\nBNLearn causal discovery.\nWe construct 100 tasks from the Alarm (32 tasks), Child (17), Insurance (24),\nand Water (27) networks in the Bayesian Network\nRepository ([Scutari, n.d.](#bib.bib2)). Each task is a randomly\npermuted, anonymized 14–34-node induced subgraph. A linear-Gaussian structural\nequation model provides 1,800 training and 600 visible validation observations.\nThe agent may analyze the visible data, query validation Bayesian information criterion (BIC) diagnostics, and, on an \\(n\\)-node task, request \\(n\\) interventions that report estimated total effects. These diagnostics do not determine reward, and the executor cannot query graph accuracy while carrying out an investigation. The final artifact is a directed acyclic graph (DAG).\nAfter each completed iteration, the harness reports directed structural Hamming distance (SHD), including missing, extra, and reversed edges. For the empty-graph SHD \\(S_{0}\\) and submitted-graph SHD \\(S_{t}\\), proxy quality is \\(q_{t}=\\operatorname{clip}((S_{0}-S_{t})/S_{0},0,1)\\). The best model-authored graph is retained and restored for final scoring under the same hidden-DAG objective. Its gold score is the normalized SHD gap closure above, evaluated on the submitted graph; the task is solved only by exact directed recovery.\nPhysics discovery.\nWe adapt DiscoverPhysics ([Wiemann et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)) into 100 anonymous\nworlds spanning 12 mechanisms and three difficulty levels. The agent receives a\nmission and topology-specific laboratory interface, but not the governing law.\nAcross 20 MIRA iterations, it may allocate 15 experiment rounds among the first\n19; the last iteration is reserved for finalization. It may validate experiment\nplans and repeatedly fit executable candidate laws to all acquired trajectories.\nThe agent selects an executable law and a short explanation. For acquired-trajectory MSE \\(m_{t}\\) and position variance \\(v_{t}\\), proxy quality is \\(q_{t}=0.95/(1+\\sqrt{m_{t}/v_{t}})\\). After the fixed horizon, the law is evaluated once on held-out trajectories and the explanation is graded once by a hosted GPT-5.5 judge using a hidden world-specific reference and rubric. Let \\(n\\) be trajectory MSE normalized by the held-out world’s ground-truth trajectory variance, and let \\(e\\) be the explanation score. For an unsolved task, the gold score is the product of the threshold-scaled components \\(0.1/\\max(n,0.1)\\) and \\(\\min(1,e/0.75)\\), capped strictly below one. A task is solved, and receives a gold score of one, when \\(n<0.1\\) and \\(e\\geq 0.75\\).\nSRBench symbolic regression.\nWe use 100 LLM-SRBench problems ([Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) from chemical\nreactions (30 tasks), physical oscillators (27), materials science (23), and\npopulation growth (20). Within each family, tasks span a frozen\nreference-expression complexity ranking. The official training data are split\ndeterministically 80/20 into visible fit and validation sets; the\nin-distribution (ID) test set, optional out-of-distribution (OOD) set, and\nreference expression remain hidden.\nThe agent may analyze the available data freely and evaluate expressions within a constrained SymPy-compatible grammar. It must select one final expression. The environment reports proxy metrics but does not restore the candidate with the highest proxy metric, so final selection remains with the agent.\nFor validation NMSE \\(N_{t}\\), fixed initial-expression NMSE \\(B\\), and float64 floor \\(\\epsilon\\), proxy quality is\n|  | \\[q_{t}=\\operatorname{clip}\\!\\left(\\frac{\\log(B/\\max\\{N_{t},\\epsilon\\})}{\\log(B/\\epsilon)},0,1\\right).\\] |  | (5) | \nThe final expression is evaluated once on sealed ID-test data, with OOD metrics reported when available. Its gold score is \\(R^{2}\\) on the sealed ID test. The task is solved when pointwise relative error is at most \\(0.1\\) everywhere (and the prediction is exact when the target is zero).\nHillClimbBench-CPU architecture research.\nWe select 50 research papers whose central architectural contribution can be implemented, trained, evaluated, and iteratively improved within a CPU-only budget, and construct one task from each paper. We run two independent trajectories per task. Each task prompt combines a technical paper summary, research brief, description of how gold evaluation differs from proxy evaluation, evaluation contract, and reference implementation. The agent tests architectural hypotheses while the host fixes the data, targets, loss, optimizer, training budget, evaluation seeds, and resource limits. Its fixed portion contains the task statement, a compact workspace summary, the latest proxy evaluation, a structured summary of the immediately preceding work episode and a pointer to its handover when available, and the remaining budget. The full episode record and handover text are not copied automatically. The fixed portion and this selected evidence form the new decision context.\nThe policy is instructed to select a bounded objective or coherent experiment set while considering its information value, cost, remaining time, and risk to the current solution. Its work order states the objective, relevant evidence, resource guidance, acceptance criteria, and stopping condition. Recorded failures and contradictory results remain available to later decisions through the repository.\nThe repository—including the current candidate, experiments, results, analyses, and handovers—persists across iterations; the model contexts do not. Any proxy evaluation produced after execution becomes evidence for the next decision. The graphs are used only for analysis and are never shown to the evaluated system.\nComparable evidence projection.\nWe project every run into the same chronological evidence format: the common task statement, text authored by the evaluated models, and the submitted proof. For MIRA, model-authored text includes the outer-loop policy’s work orders and the executors’ reports; for Codex, it includes assistant messages. We exclude commands, tool outputs, diffs, commits, repository metadata, handovers, submission reviews, and external evaluations from every condition. The same projection is applied at each forced-continuation checkpoint, and the same downstream extraction procedure is applied to every inference system.\nSemantic graph extraction.\nA GPT-5.5 pass at temperature zero converts the projected trace into a graph. A node represents a semantically meaningful proof or research step, such as an approach, claim, lemma, counterexample, experiment, candidate, result, or submission. Several raw turns may therefore map to one node, while one long response may contain several nodes. Nodes record their status, such as supported, proved, refuted, stalled, abandoned, or submitted. Typed edges record evidence-supported relationships including refinement, dependence, support, refutation, pivoting, supersession, and production of a result. Chronology alone does not create an edge.\nThe extractor receives the trajectory content but not the method name, model name, grader feedback, or final grader score. Every node and edge must cite a source segment in the projected evidence; outputs without valid provenance are rejected. The extracted nodes are ordered by first appearance in the trace. For visualization, the submission ancestry is placed on a central lane and other connected components are assigned separate lanes. Shortened labels and display coordinates affect only presentation, not the analysis.\nResearch-direction annotation.\nA separate GPT-5.5 pass at temperature zero groups the fixed semantic nodes into research directions. Lemmas, tests, repairs, and refinements remain in the same direction when they serve the same high-level plan; a materially different plan receives a new identifier, and a later return to a previous plan reuses its identifier. This pass may correct the order of fixed nodes when their provenance establishes a different first-introduction order, but it may not add, remove, split, or merge nodes. It observes node content, provenance episode identifiers, and graph relations, but not the method or model name, final grader score, grader feedback, node status, terminal submission, graph title, or graph summary.\nStrategic inertia.\nTwo consecutive non-submission semantic steps form a continuation when they belong to the same annotated research direction and a pivot otherwise. For graph \\(\\mathcal{G}\\), we define \\(I(\\mathcal{G})=N_{\\mathrm{continue}}/(N_{\\mathrm{continue}}+N_{\\mathrm{pivot}})\\). The root and terminal submission are excluded. All three campaigns use the same task reference architecture, proxy evaluator, compute budget, and parameter limit. Proxy evaluations guide research, and the final submitted architecture is scored separately by the gold evaluator.\nSelected architecture.\nAn ordinary RMT stores each token’s residual state as a matrix \\(X_{\\ell}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{D_{k}\\times D_{v}}\\). Attention and MLP branches read this state through a lower-rank interface, produce a matrix update \\(\\Delta X_{\\ell}^{(b)}\\), and add it uniformly as \\(X_{\\ell,\\mathrm{out}}^{(b)}=X_{\\ell,\\mathrm{in}}^{(b)}+\\Delta X_{\\ell}^{(b)}\\) for \\(b\\in\\{\\mathrm{attn},\\mathrm{mlp}\\}\\). MIRA’s final model retains this matrix state and low-rank read/write interface, uses a GELU-gated linear unit (GEGLU) feed-forward block, and introduces a separate learned gate for each branch and layer: \\(X_{\\ell,\\mathrm{out}}^{(b)}=X_{\\ell,\\mathrm{in}}^{(b)}+G_{\\ell}^{(b)}\\odot\\Delta X_{\\ell}^{(b)}\\), where \\(G_{\\ell}^{(b)}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{D_{k}\\times D_{v}}\\). Every coefficient is initialized to one, so the new update is exactly the ordinary additive update at initialization. The two gates add only 0.056% to the model’s parameter count. Unlike a generic GEGLU block, this mechanism is specific to the matrix residual stream: it learns where each branch can amplify or suppress a proposed matrix-state write.\nMIRA first tested the gate at small scale, then reran a final-scale comparison\nmatched in geometry, seed, optimizer, and training tokens. Relative to the\nungated GEGLU control, the gated model improved last-token cross-entropy by\n0.009 at small scale and by 0.021 at the final scale.\nBoth comparisons use one seed, and the ungated final-geometry control was not\nsubmitted to the gold evaluator. These ablations support the gate\nas a promising mechanism, but they do not isolate it as the cause of the full\ngold-loss improvement in Fig. [4](#S6.F4)(b).\nThe learned gates did not remain near their identity initialization. Attention gates generally amplify writes, whereas MLP gates generally suppress them, and both vary with depth. A two-way additive decomposition into row and column effects explains roughly three quarters of the variation in the depth-averaged attention map and nearly nine tenths in the MLP map, with stronger variation along the residual-key axis. This supports a structured, branch-, depth-, and address-dependent write-allocation interpretation. Context-window limits, compaction, sequence packing, and learner batching may further split a single logical episode into multiple samples. None of these boundaries is an environment termination. Treating them as terminal either removes future TD residuals from earlier advantages or places later rewards at the wrong temporal distance.\nLet a logical episode \\(\\tau=(\\xi_{1},\\ldots,\\xi_{T})\\) be partitioned into stored samples \\(\\mathcal{S}_{1},\\ldots,\\mathcal{S}_{K}\\). The partition does not alter the termination indicator: \\(d_{t}=1\\) only when the environment truly terminates. Each stored transition contains \\(o_{t}\\), \\(a_{t}\\), \\(r_{t}\\), \\(o_{t+1}\\), actor behavior log probabilities and version \\(\\theta^{b_{t}}\\), and the critic version \\(c_{t}\\) assigned to the transition. For that critic version, the system records both the current baseline \\(v_{t}=V_{\\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t})\\) and its successor bootstrap \\(\\widetilde{v}_{t+1}^{(c_{t})}=V_{\\theta^{c_{t}}}(o_{t+1})\\).\nAn asynchronous episode may adopt a different critic before decision \\(t+1\\).\nAt a nonterminal boundary where this occurs, we use the bridge \\(B_{t}\\) from\nSec. [5.2](#S5.SS2), and set \\(B_{t}=0\\) otherwise. Terminal successor\nvalues and bridges are zero. The implemented residual and backward recurrence\nfollow Sec. [5.2](#S5.SS2) and Eq. [2](#S5.E2).\nThe bridge aligns the outgoing critic’s successor baseline with the baseline\nrecorded by the critic version assigned to the next decision.\nAfter this episode-level pass, \\(\\hat{A}_{t}\\) is scattered back to its original sample and token span for actor training. The critic target is the Monte Carlo return \\(G_{t}\\), computed separately over the same reconstructed episode. The recorded value forecasts are treated as constants during the actor update, so the policy loss does not differentiate through the shared critic baseline.\nPartition invariance.\nFor any partition of a fixed logical episode into stored samples, stitching produces the same actor advantages and Monte Carlo critic targets as processing the unpartitioned episode, provided the logged transitions, critic versions, and successor bootstraps are preserved. Stitching restores the same ordered rewards, terminal indicators, baselines, and cross-version bridges. A backward induction on the recurrence therefore yields the same \\(\\hat{A}_{t}\\) at every decision, while every reward remains in the same Monte Carlo return.\nIn our implementation, \\(\\gamma=1\\) and the critic is trained on the exact Monte Carlo return \\(G_{t}=\\sum_{u=t}^{T}r_{u}\\). Actor advantages use episode-length-adaptive \\(\\lambda_{T}=1-1/(\\alpha T)\\), giving \\(\\hat{A}_{t}=\\delta_{t}+\\lambda_{T}(1-d_{t})(\\hat{A}_{t+1}+B_{t})\\). The full Monte Carlo return receives weight \\(\\lambda_{T}^{T-1}\\geq 1-1/\\alpha\\) at the earliest decision and greater weight at later decisions. The bridges correct baseline-coordinate changes but do not alter the Monte Carlo critic target. Stitching remains necessary: \\(G_{t}\\) must include rewards from all later MIRA iterations, even when those iterations occupy different contexts, samples, or learner batches.\nThe governing invariant is that only true environment termination may stop value bootstrapping; infrastructure boundaries never do.\nAppendix J Additional MIRA-AC Results\nAppendix K Behavioral Analysis of MIRA-AC\nThis appendix examines the changes we observe in the model’s meta-reasoning and scientific behavior as a result of training. We compare the cross-environment MIRA-AC policy with untrained MIRA on 400 matched evaluation pairs (800 trajectories): 100 pairs each from symbolic regression, Physics Discovery, HillClimbBench-CPU, and BNLearn. Each pair contains the two policies’ trajectories on the same evaluation instance. We call the held-out evaluation result the gold outcome. In both systems, an outer policy selects the next research direction, an inner executor carries it out, and the harness manages execution and can force continuation after an incomplete iteration.\nK.1 Analysis Protocol\nWe use GPT-5.5 as an LLM judge in two passes. In the first pass, the judge sees the sequence of work orders, executor reports, and proxy feedback under anonymous labels A and B, but not the policy identities or gold outcomes. It records what each trajectory’s policy and executor did, how they responded to evidence, and whether the policy’s next action could resolve an uncertainty visible at the time. This assessment is fixed before the second pass.\nIn the second pass, the judge receives this fixed assessment and each side’s gold outcome; policy identity remains hidden. It separately assesses whether a decision was reasonable when made and whether it ultimately helped or harmed the gold result. The judge also attributes each claimed contribution among the outer policy, inner executor, harness, and unobserved factors. The pair-level verdict used below asks which side’s outer-policy decisions contributed more to its own final outcome. The verdict excludes executor-only and harness-only contributions. Policy identity is revealed only after both passes are complete. All 400 pairs produced valid judgments in both passes.\nFor behavior rates, the unit is a trajectory, not an action. A trajectory is counted once for a behavior if the second-pass judge identifies at least one instance attributable at least partly to the outer policy; repeated instances do not increase the count. Behaviors attributed only to the executor, harness, or unobserved factors are excluded. The exception is productive risk, a whole-trajectory label indicating that a reasonable exploratory bet ultimately improved the gold result. Every rate therefore has a denominator of 400 trajectories per policy.\nThe matched comparisons describe observed differences between the policies. They do not estimate the effect of independently inducing a single behavior: executor stochasticity, interactions among decisions, and the evaluation environment can also affect the final result. The aggregate rates establish how common each behavior was; the excerpts below show what those behaviors looked like in individual trajectories.\nK.2 Pooled Behavioral Changes\nThe outcome-aware judge credited MIRA-AC’s outer-policy decisions with the larger contribution in 203 pairs and untrained MIRA’s in 131; 66 pairs were tied or unclear. MIRA-AC therefore received 60.8% of the 334 decisive attributions. It was also more often classified as taking a defensible risk that improved the gold result (210/400 trajectories, 52.5%, versus 139/400, 34.8%).\nThe remaining rows of Fig. [9](#S6.F9) describe what changed\nin the research process. Relative to untrained MIRA, MIRA-AC more often\nchanged its explanation of the underlying mechanism or the form of its solution\n(185/400 vs. 148/400),\nran a test that distinguished competing ideas (112/400 vs. 67/400), checked\nwhether an improvement held across conditions or edge cases (28/400 vs. 10/400), and made an evidence-supported decision about whether to continue\n(53/400 vs. 29/400). It less often repeated minor variants that yielded\nlittle new information (75/400 vs. 134/400).\nK.3 Selecting and Attributing Trajectory Examples\nFor each environment, the examples include at least one case in which MIRA-AC produced the better gold result and one in which it performed worse than untrained MIRA. We select cases only after computing the aggregate statistics across all trajectories, favoring cases with a clear chain from the available evidence to an outer decision, an executor result, and the gold outcome. Additional cases are included only when they clarify a distinct behavior or the boundary between policy and executor credit. The examples are thus illustrative rather than estimates of how common each behavior was, and every quotation is linked to its original decision or result record.\nA case in which training hurt performance is included only when it reveals an interpretable decision tendency consistent with the training feedback or data distribution, not merely because MIRA-AC lost the pair. Its discussion reconstructs why the choice was reasonable given the information available at the time, explains why the proxy evidence favored a decision that later performed poorly on the gold evaluation, and states any suggested remedy as a testable hypothesis rather than an established correction.\nA useful idea reported by the executor can still have been elicited by the outer loop. We distinguish five relationships between the outer decision and the eventual idea:\n- 1. \nDirect outer specification. The outer work order names the useful hypothesis, test, or planned response before execution.\n- 2. \nOuter-seeded executor discovery. The outer policy isolates the relevant uncertainty or asks for a test likely to resolve it. The executor then supplies the concrete mechanism or implementation.\n- 3. \nState scaffolding. Earlier outer decisions create or preserve a useful best candidate or body of evidence, but do not point the executor toward the eventual idea.\n- 4. \nExecutor-independent pivot. The executor introduces a useful direction outside the requested experiment and its stated contingencies.\n- 5. \nHarness-enabled opportunity. Forced continuation or retry makes later work possible but contributes no research hypothesis. Any later outer decision or executor discovery is credited separately.\nThese categories describe an idea’s provenance, not whether it was good. We require a specific link in content between a work order and a later discovery. An executor idea is not outer-seeded merely because the work order came first, requested a progress update, or was followed by an iteration forced by the harness.\nK.4 Symbolic Regression\nIn symbolic regression, productive research means identifying which variables and mathematical form explain the data, testing that explanation where it is most likely to fail, and resisting improvements that depend on a feature specific to the proxy validation set. Across the 100 matched tasks, MIRA-AC obtained the higher gold score in 58 cases, compared with 32 for untrained MIRA (10 ties). The LLM trajectory judge more often found that MIRA-AC ran a test designed to distinguish two explanations (14% vs. 6%), checked robustness or an edge case (12% vs. 4%), and made a well-supported decision to stop or continue (25% vs. 11%). It also found fewer premature stops (13% vs. 29%) and fewer stretches of repeated minor variants that added little information (3% vs. 13%). These rates count whether a behavior appeared at least once in a trajectory; the matched cases below illustrate how the same tendency to act on specific evidence could help or hurt.\nTesting a specific error reveals a better equation.\nIn MatSci25 (pair symbolic:047), the task asks for a compact equation for stress as a function of strain \\(\\epsilon\\) and temperature \\(T\\). The proxy fit suggested that the coefficient relating stress to strain varied with temperature, but its worst relative errors remained too large. MIRA-AC made that error the target of the next experiment:\nOuter policy (B-D002): “Scan alternative parametric forms for the temperature-dependent modulus \\(E(T)\\) in Stress \\(\\approx\\epsilon E(T)\\) to reduce max relative error and potentially achieve acc_0_1.”\nExecutor (B-R002): “epsilon and T are perfectly correlated (\\(r\\approx 1.0\\)) in the data, so fitting \\(\\epsilon^{p}E(T)\\) with \\(p\\neq 1\\) reduces MRE substantially.”\nThe resulting expression reduced the maximum relative error from 0.140 to 0.052. MIRA-AC scored 0.9949 on the gold evaluation; untrained MIRA scored 0. This was an outer-seeded executor discovery: the outer policy selected the relationship to inspect and the error criterion, which exposed the \\(\\epsilon\\)–\\(T\\) correlation, while the executor introduced the decisive generalization from \\(\\epsilon\\) to \\(\\epsilon^{p}\\). The gold result reversed the proxy ordering: untrained MIRA scored 0.9998 and MIRA-AC scored 0. Forced continuation preserved later opportunities to work, but supplied neither the candidate equation nor the selection criterion.\nInterpretation. Training did not simply make the model change the mathematical form of its answer more often: the judge attributed such a change to the outer policy, or jointly to the outer policy and executor, in 27% of MIRA-AC trajectories and 36% of untrained MIRA trajectories, but those trajectories produced the higher gold score 66.7% and 47.2% of the time, respectively. The two cases show when this tendency helps and when it hurts. A test tied to a systematic pattern in the errors can reveal a better equation; the same pressure to improve a proxy error can favor an expression that uses a constant fitted at the edge of the observed data and may not extrapolate. One testable remedy is to evaluate expressions containing such constants on held-out examples near and beyond the range seen during fitting.\nForced continuation appeared in 57 MIRA-AC trajectories and 17 untrained MIRA trajectories in this environment. This weakens a causal interpretation of the aggregate difference.\nK.5 Physics Discovery\nPhysics Discovery gives the agent a limited budget for simulator experiments and then evaluates its submitted law on hidden trajectories.
