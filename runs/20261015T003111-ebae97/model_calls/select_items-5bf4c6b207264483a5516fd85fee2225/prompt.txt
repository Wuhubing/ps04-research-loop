We score these artifacts against a paper-derived reference that is withheld from the evaluated system.\n3.1 Execution modes\nResearch in each task proceeds through seven stages in three parts: design and planning (literature review, research claims, method design, and experiment planning), experimental execution, and analysis and writing (results analysis and paper writing). Each stage delivers one artifact at a fixed location, such as a claims document with success criteria, a method specification, or the code, logs, and results of execution. We run these stages in two modes (Figure [2](#S3.F2)a).\nEnd-to-end run. The evaluated system starts from the research brief and completes all seven stages in one workspace. Each stage builds on the system’s own earlier artifacts, which it may also revise, so errors made in early stages carry into later ones.\nStagewise run. Each stage runs separately in a fresh workspace. The run for a given stage receives the research brief and the paper-derived outputs of all preceding stages, while the paper’s output for the stage itself stays hidden. A stagewise score therefore measures how well a system performs a stage when its inputs match those of the anchor paper, and comparing the two modes shows how much each stage depends on what the system itself produced upstream.\n3.2 Task construction\nWe build each task from the anchor paper and its supplementary material, and also draw on its official code repository. Going through these sources stage by stage, we record what the paper provides at each stage and trace every derived item to the evidence that supports it. All material is frozen before any system is run. A task gives the evaluated system its inputs and fixes the claims the research must establish.\nInputs. The research brief poses the research problem and its core idea. It also describes the accessible data and the engineering constraints of the project. For stagewise runs we additionally derive one input document per stage from the paper. Each document records what the paper produced at that stage, from the literature review up to the conclusion drawn for each claim (Table [4](#A1.T4) in Appendix [A](#A1)).\nClaims. Each task freezes a set of required claims. Method claims state what the method must implement, and empirical claims state what the experiments must show. Every claim carries an explicit success criterion. It also lists the claims it depends on and the extrapolations it does not license. The execution stage pursues these claims and the results analysis concludes on each of them (Table [9](#A2.T9) in Appendix [B](#A2)).\n3.3 Rubric scoring\nStage rubrics. Every stage is scored with a rubric derived from a hidden, paper-derived reference, which records for each entry the supporting evidence from the paper and its location in the official implementation. A rubric follows the structure of its stage in the reference (Table [10](#A3.T10) in Appendix [C](#A3)). Its dimensions divide the stage’s work into parts, and its items are the units the reference records within each part, such as the research areas of a literature review or the components of a method. From experimental execution onward, the items are the required claims. Experimental execution has four dimensions. Module development and code execution check that the method is implemented and the experiments are actually run, whereas result quality and claim completion are rated once per required claim. Every item states a criterion, cites the reference entries it is judged against, and names the submitted artifact expected to hold the evidence, so that the judge knows where to look. The reference, the claims, and the rubrics were reviewed by a domain researcher before any system was run.\nClaims and iteration. Experimental execution is where the required claims are tested, and two of its dimensions assess each claim directly. Result quality asks whether the evidence for a claim supports it, whereas claim completion asks how far the system carried the claim toward its criterion. Rated over all required claims, the two dimensions measure multi-claim completion, that is, how many of the claims a run carries through and how far. The judge also issues a binary verdict for each claim, met or unmet, according to whether admissible evidence satisfies its success criterion. These verdicts support iteration diagnostics. Each attempt is mapped to a required claim, and we compare the claims a system reports as attained with the claims judged met and follow each claim’s metric values across attempts (Appendix [D](#A4)). The ratings thus score how far a run advances each claim, while the verdicts and diagnostics show which claims it establishes and whether its revisions lead there, without entering any score.\nScore composition. Item ratings are averaged within a dimension and normalized to 0–100. Dimensions then combine into stage scores, which in turn combine into three research parts (Figure [2](#S3.F2)b). Stagewise runs remove this carryover, so we compare the two modes for the three harnesses (Figure [3](#S5.F3), with scores in Table [15](#A7.T15) in Appendix [G](#A7)). At every stage, all three harnesses score higher in stagewise runs than in end-to-end runs. The gains are largest in experimental execution and results analysis. Within execution, most of the gain comes from result quality and claim completion rather than code execution, and the number of claims that meet their success criteria rises from one to three in end-to-end runs to seven or eight in stagewise runs (Table [16](#A7.T16) in Appendix [G](#A7)). Much of the end-to-end execution gap thus appears to originate upstream of execution.\n5.2 Performance within experimental execution\nResult quality is the weakest execution dimension, and few claims meet their criteria. To find which part of execution holds its score down, we decompose it into its four dimensions (execution columns of Table [2](#S4.T2)). Averaged over the nine systems, result quality is the lowest dimension (26.7), 31.2 points below module development (57.9). Building an implementation is thus much easier for these systems than producing evidence that meets the task’s scientific criteria. The per-claim verdicts show the same pattern (Table [16](#A7.T16) in Appendix [G](#A7)). Across the nine end-to-end runs, only 14 of the 90 required claims meet their success criteria, and no system meets more than four. Claim completion is also low, averaging 40.8 and ranging from 0.0 to 65.0. Even Kimi K3, which reaches the highest claim completion (65.0, tied with GPT-6 Astra), meets the criteria of only three of its ten claims.\nNeither self-reports nor repeated attempts track claim attainment. An autoresearch system decides for itself when a claim is established, so we compare, over execution time, the claims each system reports as successful with those the evaluator confirms as met (Figures [4](#S5.F4)a and [4](#S5.F4)b; Appendix [D](#A4)). GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-6 Astra each report success on eight claims, but only three and four meet their criteria. The met count of GPT-5.6 Sol reaches three 0.26 hours into execution and rises no further, while its reported count climbs from three to eight between 0.76 and 1.52 hours. We then ask whether repeated attempts close this gap, following each claim’s metric values across consecutive attempts that share the same metric definition (Appendix [D](#A4)). On the main decoding claim (E1), which requires the decoder to outperform every baseline on all three subjects (Table [9](#A2.T9) in Appendix [B](#A2)), GPT-5.6 Sol raises its session-mean \\(R^{2}\\) on subject M1 from 0.49 to 0.54 over six successive attempts (Figure [4](#S5.F4)c), whereas ARIS, DeepScientist, and Kimi K3 end within 0.015 of their first attempt (Figure [6](#A4.F6) in Appendix [D](#A4)). None of the four meets the criterion. Repeated attempts thus change the numbers but rarely turn an unmet claim into a met one, and a system’s own account of its progress is an unreliable guide to which claims are established.\n5.3 Effects of skill augmentation and research orchestration\nNeither skill augmentation nor research orchestration closes the execution gap. To test whether research-specific structure improves on a general-purpose harness with the same model, we compare Codex with GPT-5.5 against the two skill-augmented harnesses and the two research-orchestration systems, all of which use GPT-5.5 as their backbone model (Table [2](#S4.T2)). ARIS and Orchestra reach overview scores within one point of Codex alone (44.5 and 45.2 vs. 45.1), but this parity hides a trade-off. They score 15.0 to 20.0 points higher on paper writing (52.5 and 47.5 vs. 32.5), yet lower in execution, where result quality drops from 30.0 to 20.0 for both and claim completion from 42.5 to 32.5 and 37.5. Each also reports success on eight claims within half an hour of starting execution but meets only one criterion (Figures [4](#S5.F4)a and [4](#S5.F4)b). ARIS logs 28 attempts (Table [16](#A7.T16) in Appendix [G](#A7)), the most among the harnesses, without meeting more claims than Codex alone. The research-orchestration systems fall further behind in execution. DeepScientist scores highest among the GPT-5.5 systems on all four design stages, yet its code execution is half that of Codex alone (25.0 vs. 50.0). Its result quality is 0.0, and none of its 34 attempts meets a criterion. AutoResearchClaw’s generated code crashed on a missing import before any scientific attempt, which leaves it with the lowest overview score (18.6). Added skills thus improve how the work is written up more than how it is carried out, and research-specific orchestration does not improve execution either.\n5.4 Resource use and performance\nScores tend to rise with run time and token use. To test whether higher scores simply reflect longer runs and more tokens, we plot the overview score against wall-clock time and total tokens (Figure [5](#S5.F5), with per-run values in Table [13](#A6.T13) in Appendix [F](#A6)). Overall, systems that run longer and consume more tokens tend to score higher. All four runs shorter than one hour score at most 45.2, whereas four of the five longer runs score above 59. Likewise, three of the four runs that use more than 50 M tokens score above 59, compared with one of the five runs that use fewer than 20 M. Across the nine runs, the Spearman correlation with the overview score is 0.47 for time and 0.23 for tokens, and it rises to 0.79 and 0.57 without DeepScientist. The main exception is DeepScientist, which runs for 7.0 hours and uses the most tokens of any system (205.7 M), yet scores only 39.9. More resources thus accompany higher scores on average but do not guarantee them.\n6 Discussion and conclusion\nResearchFlow-Eval evaluates autoresearch as one continuous workflow, following each required claim from design through experimental evidence and grounding every score in rubrics derived from a published paper. This view exposes an execution gap that scores on isolated tasks or final artifacts would miss. Experimental execution scores below design and planning in every run, and the gap is widest at the level of claims. Few required claims meet their success criteria, yet systems often report success on claims that do not. Neither skill augmentation nor research-specific orchestration closes this gap. Because a system’s own account of its progress does not reveal which claims are established, future systems may benefit more from checking each claim against an explicit criterion than from relying on their own progress reports. Our study also has limitations. Because the rubrics follow the anchor paper, they may undervalue scientifically valid alternatives to its design. In addition, the literature cutoff restricts what a system can retrieve during a run but does not rule out exposure to the anchor paper during pretraining. In future work, we will apply ResearchFlow-Eval to additional autoresearch systems, models, and anchor papers.\nEthics statement\nThis work evaluates research artifacts produced by autoresearch systems. It suggests a transformer-style set decoder that reads binned spike counts through cross-attention. Each unit token covers one causal time window, and its identity is inferred from a small amount of unlabeled activity in the new session. The model is meant to be shallow and fast. Inputs are causal binned neural observations, and outputs are last-step motor covariates. These suggestions constrain the search without prescribing an implementation, so the task evaluates research carried out under these priors rather than invention from an unspecified idea.\nData and test access.\nTrain, validation, and test sessions may all be used while optimizing a claim. Calibration to a new session may read its neural activity but not its behavior labels, and it may not update model parameters. The primary metric is per-session multi-output variance-weighted \\(R^{2}\\), with negative values preserved. Because the sessions are selected locally from the public data, these scores are not comparable to the private FALCON leaderboard. Latency is processing time divided by the duration of the decoded data. A ratio below one is an offline result, not evidence of closed-loop deployment.\nRequired claims.\nTable [9](#A2.T9) lists the ten required claims. The events mark the start and end of the stage, the start of each claim, and the start and completion of each attempt. They also record the diagnosis after a failure with the revision applied, a claim that the system considers attained or reopened, and a budget stop or dependency block. These events are the system’s own account. The evaluator keeps them as reported and does not judge whether a revision was substantive. The recorder adds a sequence number and a hash chain, so earlier events cannot be rewritten without detection. The runtime observes the log from outside the workspace and adds the timestamps, so the evaluated system cannot write elapsed times or token usage. For the empirical claims, every completed attempt must report its raw metric values together with a pointer to the result file. Each value carries the metadata that identify it, such as its metric, split, and comparator. The runtime stores these values unchanged and computes no improvement or best-so-far quantity.\nReported and confirmed progress.\nAfter the run, the evaluator maps each attempt to a required claim, whether the attempt was logged through the recorder or is recoverable from the system’s native records. The reported track in Figure [4](#S5.F4)(a) counts a claim when the evaluated system first reports it as attained. The confirmed track in Figure [4](#S5.F4)(b) places each met claim at the first attempt whose evidence the judge finds sufficient. Times are measured from the start of experimental execution and record when an event was logged, not when the underlying computation ran. Table [16](#A7.T16) in Appendix [G](#A7) lists the final number of reported and met claims and the number of attempts for every run.\nMetric trajectories.\nA claim-specific series collects the metric values that share one full metric identity. This identity fixes the claim and the metric with its unit and direction. It also fixes the data slice and the aggregation, the comparison against a threshold, and the evaluation protocol with its varied scientific factor. Each attempt contributes at most one point to a series, namely the mean of its replicates. Only adjacent points of the same series are connected, in the order of their logged times. For each of the four systems displayed in Figures [4](#S5.F4)(c) and [6](#A4.F6), the panels show the longest audited chain on the main decoding claim. Kimi K3 has no chain longer than two attempts. The diagnostics receive zero weight in every score.\nAppendix E Judge protocol, rating anchors, and integrity gates\nJudge calls.\nAll ratings come from claude-opus-5, called through the Claude Code CLI with the model pinned. Each call draws one sample, with no fallback model and no selection or averaging across samples. Temperature and seed are not exposed by the interface. Calls run serially. A workflow receives nine calls. The four design and planning stages and results analysis each receive one call. Paper writing receives two. The first assesses scientific writing and traceability to earlier stages. The second reads only the rendered paper.pdf and reviews it on five conference criteria, from soundness to reproducibility. Experimental execution also receives two. The stage call rates module development and code execution. A separate claim-level call rates result quality and claim completion for all ten claims and issues their met or unmet verdicts.\nIn each call, the judge reads the scoring profile and the stage rubric with the reference entries that each item cites. It also reads the frozen submission and the upstream artifacts visible to that stage, plus operator logs when attempt facts need confirmation. The submission is treated as untrusted data, so instructions or self-assessments written into it do not change the rubric. For every item, the judge returns an integer rating with a short rationale. It also returns the locations of the supporting evidence, the reference entries used, and any gate findings. Runner exit codes, self-reported claim states, and the system’s own success flags carry no weight by themselves.\nExample item.\nOne item of the experiment-planning rubric asks the plan to freeze an executable experiment contract for the robustness claim about unit loss. Its criterion cites the corresponding section of the paper-derived reference and the experiment unit derived from it, and it expects the evidence in the submitted experiment plan. The item lists twelve required facets. They fix what the experiment tests, how its result is judged against the frozen claim standard, and how it is run and stopped, for example the retention conditions, the comparator, and the stop rule. A rating of 0 means that the plan is missing, wrong, or cannot test the claim. A rating of 1 means that it gives only an experiment name or goal, with no conditions, controls, or evidence contract. A rating of 2 means that the direction exists but several conditions or controls are not frozen. A rating of 3 means that the plan is executable except for one limited gap. A rating of 4 means that all listed facets are complete and faithful to the frozen claim standard. The required facets are coverage checks that the judge must consider, not sub-weights.\nRating anchors.\nTable [11](#A5.T11) gives the anchors of the four execution dimensions. In claim completion, a rating of 2 marks substantive work that lacks a key protocol element, comparator, or piece of evidence. A rating of 3 marks a complete and adjudicable execution whose result misses the criterion. A rating of 4 requires both a complete execution and a met criterion. A well-executed experiment with a negative result therefore still earns 3. The anchors of the other stages have the same form and are adapted to each item. In results analysis, a claim whose result is failed or mixed can receive at most 3, so that the top rating requires both the expected outcome and a complete analysis.\n| Rating | Module development | Code execution | Result quality | Claim completion | \n|---|---|---|---|---|\n| 0 | Missing, fundamentally wrong, or unable to satisfy the method claim | No credible execution | No admissible result, or a key element such as the comparator or provenance is missing | No corresponding work, no execution, or invalid evidence | \n| 1 | Only stubs, interfaces, or module names | Only scripts and configurations, or claims of execution without support | A complete, valid experiment fails to support or contradicts the claim | Only implementation, a smoke test, or an initial attempt | \n| 2 | Substantive code with an incomplete key mechanism or data flow | The real path was run and left credible partial evidence, but the key path is unfinished | Real and admissible but mixed or weak support | Substantive work lacking a key protocol element, comparator, or piece of evidence | \n| 3 | Core implementation correct, with one limited gap in interface, tests, or integration | Run completed, with one limited protocol or evidence gap | Credible result that mostly meets the criterion, with the unmet parts stated | Complete, adjudicable execution whose result misses the criterion | \n| 4 | Complete and correct implementation, with enough code or tests to support the judgment | Qualified run with correct protocol and controls, and with its commands and logs | Admissible result that meets the criterion with correctly bounded scope | Qualified execution whose admissible evidence meets the criterion | \nClaim verdicts.\nThe judge maps the evaluated system’s own records onto the ten required claims, since these records may use different claim names or combine several required claims in one experiment. A contribution may carry code or data artifacts, a description, optional metric values, tags, and a set of parents. At any moment the platform should be able to answer four questions:\n- 1. \nWhat has been tried, including failures?\n- 2. \nWhich claims have independent support or conflict?\n- 3. \nWhere is the current frontier, including neglected alternatives?\n- 4. \nWhat exact artifact and lineage produced a reported result?\nTable [1](#S3.T1) turns these into system goals.\n| Goal | Failure without a shared institution | Agora mechanism | \n| Durable memory | Session-local discoveries and negative results disappear. | Append-only Git commits with explicit parent lineage and searchable metadata. | \n| Frontier visibility | Workers guess what is open and duplicate the same branch. | Leaf, hypothesis, verification, cluster, and metric-landscape views. | \n| Evidence quality | Votes reward popularity; self-citation and repeated endorsement are cheap. | Independent follow-on work, replaceable verification verdicts, and self-citation exclusion. | \n| Search diversity | A leaderboard concentrates all workers on one local basin. | Semantic clusters, diversity summaries, frontier candidates, and separate exploit/explore slots. | \n| Auditability | A scalar score loses the configuration and code that produced it. | Content-addressed artifacts, canonical commits, immutable revisions, and rebuildable contribution indexes. | \nProjects define their own instructions, metrics, artifact requirements, and safety boundaries. Code-bearing contributions carry the full repository state. Git hashes identify immutable commits, and Git parentage makes their graph acyclic. A contribution’s evidence score is the\nweighted count of what other accounts built on it,\n|  | \\[S(u)=\\sum_{v:(u,v)\\in E^{*}}\\mathbbm{1}[a(u)\\neq a(v)]\\,w(v).\\] |  | (2) | \nAlongside \\(S(u)\\), each node reports an unweighted descendant count: the number of contributions by other accounts reachable from \\(u\\) through \\(E^{*}\\), excluding endorsements, work in progress, and failed verifications. Acceptance of a result depends on the project’s evaluator, controls, and artifact policy.\n3.3 Reading the frontier with analyze\nThe graph is only useful if a worker can read it quickly. A leaderboard\nhighlights the best scores but says nothing about failed attempts, claims\nthat nobody has checked, or directions nobody has tried. Agora therefore\nexposes the frontier through analyze, a CLI command\n(agora analyze) and HTTP endpoint that summarizes the current\ngraph in several views, each answering one of the questions from\nSection [3.1](#S3.SS1):\n- • \nWhat has been tried? Recent activity, the tags in use, and the contributors, so a worker can see the shape of the project before choosing a parent.\n- • \nWhich claims hold? Unverified results, contested verifications, and open hypotheses, so a worker can pick something to reproduce or test rather than only something to extend.\n- • \nWhere is the frontier? Metric leaders, the most built-on nodes by descendant count, promising but underexplored results with at most two descendants, and leaves with no follow-on work, so a worker can choose between refining the best result and picking up a neglected one.\nEvery view is computed from the Git history and the contribution index, so\nit can be rebuilt from the published record alone. analyze is the\nfirst thing each worker session runs and the last thing it consults before\npublishing (Section [4.3](#S4.SS3)), which makes it the main channel\nthrough which workers coordinate without talking to one another.\n3.4 Diversity-aware attention allocation\nThe views above still share a weakness with the leaderboard: a worker that\npicks the best-scoring parent tends to make the same choice as every other\nworker, and the graph fills with variations on one idea while alternatives\ngo untouched. Seeing this concentration requires a notion of which\ncontributions are about the same idea. When a contribution is published,\nthe server embeds its description with Arctic-Embed 2.0\n([Yu et al., 2024](#bib.bib52)), a 568M-parameter model producing 1,024-dimensional\nvectors served through an OpenAI-compatible embeddings endpoint, and stores\nthe vector in the contribution index. Once at least\n\\(50\\%\\) of contributions have embeddings, analyze groups\ndescriptions into single-link clusters, joining two contributions when the\ncosine similarity of their embeddings is at least a threshold, \\(0.90\\) by\ndefault, and caps pairwise analysis at the 5,000 most recent contributions.\nDescriptions that embed raw scores, such as\nval_bpb=1.9158…, keep near-identical experiments apart at\n\\(0.90\\), so the run in Section [4](#S4) used \\(0.80\\).\nFrom the clusters it reports how concentrated activity is. With \\(k\\) clusters holding shares \\(p_{1},\\dots,p_{k}\\) of the contributions, the top-cluster share is \\(\\max_{i}p_{i}\\), and the effective cluster count is \\(k_{\\mathrm{eff}}=\\exp(-\\sum_{i}p_{i}\\log p_{i})\\), the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the shares. It equals \\(k\\) when all clusters are the same size and falls toward \\(1\\) as one cluster dominates, so it reads as “how many ideas are really being pursued.” Evenness is \\(k_{\\mathrm{eff}}/k\\). analyze also reports cluster sizes, a histogram of metric values, and a frontier of promising nodes in small clusters.\nThe same embeddings feed candidate ranking. Candidates are ranked by a\ndiversity-aware upper-confidence bound in the spirit of bandit and\ntree-search selection rules ([Auer et al., 2002](#bib.bib29); [Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006](#bib.bib30)),\n|  | \\[U(v)=100\\,Q(v)+C\\sqrt{\\frac{\\log(N+1)}{n(v)+1}}+\\frac{100D}{\\sqrt{1+\\rho(v)}},\\] |  | (3) | \nwhere \\(Q(v)\\in[0,1]\\) is the percentile rank of \\(v\\)’s metric among the candidate results, hypotheses, and insights, in the direction of the project’s objective, with tied values sharing a percentile and candidates without a metric sharing the lowest; \\(n(v)\\) is the descendant count of \\(v\\), \\(N\\) is the number of follow-on contributions in the project, and \\(\\rho(v)\\) is the number of other contributions whose description embedding lies within cosine similarity \\(0.95\\) of \\(v\\)’s, a stricter threshold than the one used for clustering. A contribution without an embedding has \\(\\rho(v)=0\\) and receives the full bonus \\(100D\\). The diversity weight \\(D\\) defaults to \\(0.5\\) and is set to zero when embedding coverage is below \\(50\\%\\), so the third term favors ideas few others have described. The deployment we exported after the run used \\(D=1.0\\); worker reports confirm its \\(0.80\\) clustering threshold during the run, but not \\(D\\). The exploration constant \\(C\\) defaults to \\(15\\). Once the project has at least 20 follow-on contributions and 20 metric values, \\(C\\) is multiplied by \\(1+2c\\), where \\(c=\\min\\{1,\\max\\{0,\\;1-|m_{0.99}-m_{0.90}|/(0.01\\,(m_{0.95}-m_{0.05}))\\}\\}\\) and \\(m_{q}\\) is the \\(q\\)-quantile of the metric values. \\(C\\) therefore grows by up to a factor of three when the upper tail of the metric distribution is narrow. For a minimized metric such as bpb, that tail holds the weakest results rather than the best. Section [4.8](#S4.SS8) describes how we\nchecked it.\n4.1 Task and evaluator\nPretrained language models store a great deal of knowledge in their weights, but reusing it in a new architecture normally means training on data. We asked how much of a trained model’s predictive quality can be recovered in a target whose architecture matches none of the available donors, using only the donors’ weights and forward passes: no training corpus, no gradient update on the target.\nThe donor zoo holds 141 open-weight models (534 GB) from 32 architecture\nfamilies, including GPT-2, LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, Pythia, RWKV, and\nMamba\n([Radford et al., 2019](#bib.bib34); [Touvron et al., 2023](#bib.bib35); [Jiang et al., 2023](#bib.bib36); [Bai et al., 2023](#bib.bib48); [Gemma Team, 2024](#bib.bib49); [Biderman et al., 2023](#bib.bib50); [Peng et al., 2023](#bib.bib51); [Gu and Dao, 2023](#bib.bib43)).\nThe target is a 14-layer hybrid that alternates attention blocks\n([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib44)) with simplified Mamba-style state-space (SSM)\nblocks ([Gu and Dao, 2023](#bib.bib43)) that keep Mamba’s projections, depthwise causal\nconvolution, and gating but replace the selective scan with a data-dependent\nscalar gate, with hidden size 672, seven query heads sharing one key–value\nhead, untied embeddings, and 119,572,320 parameters. We chose these dimensions so\nthat no donor matches any of them. A participant submits a Python file with a\ntransfer(model, config) function that receives the randomly\ninitialized target and returns it with new weights. The evaluator seeds all\nrandom-number generators with 42, runs transfer(), and scores 200\nFineWeb-Edu texts ([Penedo et al., 2024](#bib.bib37)) in non-overlapping 512-token\nchunks under the GPT-2 tokenizer, reporting bits per byte: the summed\nnext-token cross-entropy, converted to bits, divided by the UTF-8 byte count. The FineWeb-Edu loader raises an error if called from\ninside transfer(), and the rules forbid pretraining, fine-tuning, and\nediting the evaluator or target configuration. Two runs of the same code on\nthe same hardware are bit-identical; across GPU types the score can differ\nin the third decimal place. Random initialization scores 3.3923 bpb and a\nconventionally trained GPT-2 124M about 1.0. We use the trained model as a\nreference when reporting the fraction of this gap closed by transfer.\nThe project README set an aspirational target below 2.5.\n4.2 Agents, harness, and tools\nThe workers were coding-agent sessions running frontier language models:\nClaude Code ([Anthropic, 2026a](#bib.bib2)) with Claude Opus 4.7\n([Anthropic, 2026b](#bib.bib40)) and Codex ([OpenAI, 2026a](#bib.bib1)) with GPT-5.5\n([OpenAI, 2026c](#bib.bib41)) or GPT-5.4 ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib42)). Its code is a chain of 73 Python modules, each importing its parent\nand modifying the model the parent returns; ten earlier variants that the\nchain imports for shared code bring the import closure to 83 modules. We\ntraced the imports to the base module and checked the constants against the\ncode.\nStage A builds the initialization from what the donors predict rather than\nfrom their parameters. Six donors sharing the GPT-2 vocabulary, GPT-2 small\nand large ([Radford et al., 2019](#bib.bib34)) and Cerebras-GPT 111M to 1.3B\n([Dey et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)), are queried on every vocabulary token \\(v\\) under 28\ncontexts \\(p\\): no context, end-of-text, 22 single tokens (punctuation and\nfunction words such as ’ the’), and four two-token clause starters\n(’. The’, ’, and’, ’. This’, ’, or’).\nTheir next-token log-softmaxes are blended with fixed donor weights (0.725 on\nGPT-2 small; Appendix [B](#A2) lists every constant) and per-row\ncontext weights, giving a\n\\(50257\\times 50257\\) context-averaged bigram table \\(M\\). Its column mean \\(u\\) is\nsplit off as a unigram-like anchor, and the centered table is factorized to\nrank \\(d{-}1=671\\) by randomized SVD ([Halko et al., 2011](#bib.bib45)) with a fixed\nsketch and one power iteration. The factors become the input embedding and\noutput head, hidden dimension 0 carries \\(u\\), two temperatures rescale the two\nterms, and every sublayer is zeroed. Every normalization keeps its module\nbut gets unit gain and zero bias. The final RMSNorm therefore rescales an\nembedding row by about \\(\\sqrt{d}\\), which the \\(1/\\sqrt{d}\\) factors in\nAlgorithm [1](#alg1) cancel, so for input token \\(v\\) the logits are\napproximately \\(u/T_{u}\\) plus the rank-\\((d{-}1)\\) approximation of\n\\(C(v,\\cdot)/T_{b}\\). The result is a factorized bigram model stored in a\n14-layer network.\nStage B re-enables sublayers with sparse deterministic edits on\n96-dimensional bands of the hidden state, \\(B_{k}=[1+96k,\\,97+96k)\\) for\n\\(k=0,\\dots,5\\) and \\(B_{6}=[576,672)\\), which is shifted down by one to fit and\nshares dimension 576 with \\(B_{5}\\). \\(B_{0}\\) holds the leading singular directions\n(Table [3](#S4.T3)). Every\nattention layer becomes a uniform causal mean-pool over one band written back\nat a small scale, so the model sees an average of its past embeddings.\nLayer 0’s SwiGLU block receives SVD-projected slices of GPT-2 small’s first\nMLP at scale 0.009. In each SSM block the data-dependent gate is zeroed to a\nconstant, so it reduces to a gated depthwise causal convolution over one\nband. Layer 1’s kernel has positive taps on the three preceding positions\nand a negative tap on the current one; the other layers’ kernels are\nuniform. Every SSM layer writes into \\(B_{2}\\); layers 1 and 3 also write back\ninto the band they read, and layers 1, 3, and 7 into \\(B_{3}\\)\n(Table [3](#S4.T3)). Before the run, we defined the task,\nassembled the donor zoo and the target architecture, wrote the evaluator and\nthe project brief, created the project, and launched the workers. During the\nrun we changed the server three times and the H100 workers’ prompt once. On May 2, when the analysis views\nshowed that more than a third of all activity belonged to a single semantic\ncluster and the leaderboard had stalled, we deployed the clustering, diversity summary, and diversity-aware\nUCB of Section [3.4](#S3.SS4). We set the clustering threshold for\nthis project to \\(0.80\\) instead of the default \\(0.90\\); at \\(0.90\\) the largest\ncluster would have held about 8% of activity, so both that diagnosis and\nthe new prompt’s 30% trigger below depend on this setting. That evening we stopped the H100\nlauncher at 19:13 UTC and restarted it at 21:20 UTC with a new prompt. It\ntold each session to read the landscape first and, if the largest cluster\nheld more than 30% of activity, to prefer the explore-novel slot or the\nfrontier over that cluster; to choose from the exploit, explore-known, and\nexplore-novel slots; and to post an insight explaining any idea that\nfailed. The H100 workers began citing the new views at once: on May 3,\n86% of their descriptions mention the diversity summary or the suggestion\nslots, against under 1% before the change. The A100 workers’ prompt is not\nrecorded. Their citations rose gradually instead, from 26% on May 3 to\n66% on May 5, and fell to 29% by May 8, which suggests they learned of the\nviews from analyze and other workers’ posts. The immediate shift\ntherefore reflects the prompt as much as the views, and the record cannot\nseparate the two. At 23:12 UTC, an H100 worker citing the cluster summary\npublished the first positive SSM result: a layer-1 band mean-pool that\nimproved a bare bigram baseline to 1.9297 bpb. On May 3 at 00:13 UTC,\nanother H100 worker, in a GPT-5.4 session, exploring the sparsely populated\nstate-space cluster added that block to the leading chain, scoring\n1.9028 bpb, a new best. The\nselected milestones in Table [4](#S4.T4) subsequently reached\n1.8995 bpb on May 5.\nThe other two server changes fixed faults. On May 3 we fixed the DAG endpoint,\nwhich until then had returned at most 200 contributions and dropped every\nedge whose parent fell outside that set, and added the new views to the web\ninterface, pausing the H100 workers from 14:56 to 15:24 UTC while we\nredeployed. On May 5 a worker posted a hypothesis with a placeholder metric\nof \\(-1\\), which the metric-leader views ranked first. That afternoon we\ncommitted a server change that rejects metric values on contributions\ntagged only as hypotheses, enforcing the rule in Table [2](#S3.T2); the\nrecord does not show when it went live, but no other hypothesis in the run\ncarries a metric. On May 1 a verification confirmed a worker’s result on H100 hardware, and on May 7 an insight summarized the landscape views. We also checked the reproduction records for the\nwinning method and its ancestors, which reported agreement across hardware\nto within about \\(10^{-3}\\) bpb (Section [4.5](#S4.SS5)). A verification\nrecord names its target and verdict, but the evaluator and hardware appear\nonly in its free text, and reproduction attempts that were never posted\nleft no trace. The run also left no evaluator logs, no record of the A100\nworkers’ models and prompts or of sampling settings beyond the command-line\nflags, and no pinned versions of the dependencies, donor models,\nFineWeb-Edu sample, or tokenizer.\nTo reconstruct the method, we followed the winning commit’s imports from\nthe final edit to the base module and read each module. No step accessed the\nevaluation data or used gradient descent to update a parameter.\nAlgorithm [1](#alg1) and Table [3](#S4.T3) describe that code.\nWe did not rerun the winning method; the primary result is the archived\nevaluator output, corroborated by the agents’ cross-hardware reproductions.\n5 Conclusion\nAgora is inspired by how communities of human researchers collectively advance a research frontier: they share findings, learn from unsuccessful attempts, verify claims, and build on one another’s work. Agora brings this process to asynchronous human and AI participants through a shared research record backed by Git. Contributions preserve experiments, hypotheses, insights, negative results, and verifications together with their artifacts and lineage. Multiple views of this record reveal complementary aspects of the frontier: leading results, open questions, claims awaiting verification, and promising but underexplored directions. These views help participants understand what the community has learned, identify where they can contribute, and choose work that pushes the frontier further.\nOur weight-transfer experiment provides one concrete demonstration of this approach. Over nearly 12 days, 13 agent workers independently chose experiments. They built on shared contributions to develop an initialization method that reduced the target model’s development score from 3.39 to 1.899 bits per byte, without training data or gradient updates on the target model. The resulting record contains 1,703 contributions and 165 verifications by accounts other than the targets’ authors, documenting how findings were extended, checked, and combined across participants. This case demonstrates sustained collaborative research through Agora; establishing its effect on discovery efficiency requires matched comparisons across tasks.\nAgora’s broader contribution is a foundation for research that accumulates across participants and sessions. We apply Andrej Karpathy’s AutoResearch paradigm [[1](#bib.bib1)]—a large language model that iteratively edits a training script and retains modifications that improve a held-out scalar metric—to automate this exploration. We report on twelve weeks of running this paradigm at production scale, where iterations consume hours of multi-GPU compute, evaluation involves competing criteria, and campaigns span weeks across many training jobs. Across two independently developed representation-learning systems for a book recommendation pipeline, we ran 220+ experiments and observed five recurring failure modes absent from the original setting: infrastructure fragility, agent memory decay, search-direction stagnation, iteration-cost asymmetry, and metric fixation. We contribute a three-principle scaffolding design—prevent, persist, redirect—that maps each failure mode to a structural remedy and whose instantiation scales with iteration cost. The framework produced a 1.82\\(\\times\\) Recall@6 lift and a 2.1\\(\\times\\) coherence lift over hand-tuned baselines, and the agent autonomously designed a text-only fallback that expanded catalog coverage by 5.8\\(\\times\\). The two systems span nearly three orders of magnitude in per-iteration cost yet exhibit the same failure modes, suggesting these are structural properties of production-scale autonomous research rather than artifacts of either application.\nIndex Terms:\nLLM agents, autonomous machine learning research, recommender systems, embedding learning, production machine learning, multi-agent systems, contrastive learning, agent failure modes\nI Introduction\nAndrej Karpathy’s AutoResearch [[1](#bib.bib1)]—a 630-line Python script—introduced an elegant paradigm for autonomous machine learning research: a large language model iteratively modifies a training script, runs the experiment, and keeps or discards based on a held-out scalar metric. Unlike AutoML approaches [[2](#bib.bib22)] that pick from a predefined search space, AutoResearch proposes and modifies arbitrary code: architecture, optimizer, loss function, training tricks. Applied to single-GPU, five-minute language-modeling experiments, it demonstrated that code-level iterative search can discover meaningful improvements without human intervention.\nThe paradigm has obvious appeal for production ML, where exploration budgets are large but engineering attention is scarce [[3](#bib.bib25)]. Yet production ML differs from the original setting along three axes that strain the paradigm’s assumptions. First, production scale often results in iterations consuming hours of multi-GPU compute rather than minutes, raising the cost of any failure by three orders of magnitude. Second, evaluation involves complex, competing criteria rather than a single scalar, so “keep if metric improved” is no longer sufficiently defined. Third, campaigns span weeks across many training jobs, exceeding any single context window. Whether AutoResearch survives these conditions has, to our knowledge, not been reported.\nThis paper reports on twelve weeks of running AutoResearch at production compute scale within Amazon’s book recommendation pipeline. We deployed the paradigm in two independent systems with very different cost regimes. System A learns 128-dimensional purchase optimized retrieval embeddings for 98% of the active catalog on multiple GPU instances (consuming 9–19 hour iterations); the agent rewrites the entire training script per iteration. System B learns hierarchical Semantic IDs for books via Residual Quantization (RQ-VAE) [[4](#bib.bib7), [5](#bib.bib8)] used for generative retrieval [[6](#bib.bib6)] and taxonomy on a single GPU (6–52 minute iterations); the agent edits hyperparameters and model architecture rather than the full script. The systems span nearly three orders of magnitude in per-iteration cost and were developed independently by different workstreams, which makes their shared findings more compelling. Both systems produced significant improvements over hand-tuned baselines: 1.82\\(\\times\\) Recall@6 lift on System A, and 2.1\\(\\times\\) lift on the custom evaluation metric used for System B. In terms of performance, there was a 5.8\\(\\times\\) catalog-coverage scale-up on System A, designed and implemented by the agent itself with no human direction.\nRelated work. Several recent systems explore autonomous experimentation and iterative LLM-driven code search. The AI Scientist [[7](#bib.bib2)] targets full-paper authorship via batch-style hypothesis generation and writeup, working at a coarser granularity than AutoResearch. ScientistOne [[8](#bib.bib26)] extends this line toward end-to-end autonomous research with verifiable evidence chains, but is evaluated on benchmark research tasks where iteration is cheap and the output is a paper rather than a production system. MLAgentBench [[9](#bib.bib3)] evaluates language agents on standardized small-scale ML tasks, focusing on benchmarking rather than deployment. Self-Refine [[10](#bib.bib15)] and Reflexion [[11](#bib.bib16)] formalize self-correction loops where an LLM critiques and revises its own output, but operate within a single context window. LLMs as Optimizers [[12](#bib.bib4)] and EvoPrompting [[13](#bib.bib5)] treat the LLM as a black-box optimizer over prompts or architectures. FunSearch [[14](#bib.bib17)] couples a language model with an evolutionary loop for mathematical discovery. AIDE [[15](#bib.bib24)] frames ML engineering as tree-search over code and achieves strong benchmark performance; SWE-agent [[16](#bib.bib23)] builds specialized agent-computer interfaces for repository-level code tasks. Both report on structured benchmark settings rather than production deployment. Multi-agent coordination frameworks such as ReAct [[17](#bib.bib18)] and AutoGen [[18](#bib.bib19)] address agent communication and tool use. MemGPT [[19](#bib.bib20)] addresses context-length limits via hierarchical memory management. None of these systems reports what happens when iteration cost is high, evaluation is multi-criteria, and a campaign exceeds a single context. Our work occupies that gap.\nContributions. This paper makes three contributions: (1) a taxonomy of five failure modes of AutoResearch that emerge at production scale—infrastructure fragility, agent memory decay, search-direction stagnation, iteration-cost asymmetry, and metric fixation—each with an identified root cause, with the first four independently observed across two systems of very different cost profiles and the fifth problematic only to multi-criteria evaluation; (2) a three-principle scaffolding design — prevent (pre-execution semantic gate), persist (durable cross-job memory), redirect (stagnation-triggered strategic reframing) — that maps each failure mode to a structural remedy, instantiated as a three-agent extension of AutoResearch and validated against a 1.82× Recall@6 lift and a 2.1× coherence lift across the two systems; and (3) a cost-dependence principle: the weight placed on each scaffolding principle should scale with iteration cost - pre-execution gates justify their overhead only when iterations are expensive; post-execution revert suffices when they are cheap. This principle, observed empirically across two systems differing from each other by nearly three orders of magnitude in cost, offers a concrete design parameter for teams deploying autonomous research agents beyond the settings studied here. We note that the individual mechanisms we employ—persistent memory, critique loops, pre-execution gating—are deliberately standard; the contribution is not a new algorithm but a failure taxonomy grounded in production deployment and a cost-dependence principle governing when each mechanism earns its overhead.\nII Production Setting\nBoth systems share the standard AutoResearch components [[1](#bib.bib1)]: 1) an orchestrator, a research program (program.md) carrying instructions and accumulated findings; 2) an agent sandbox containing the training script (train.py), an immutable evaluator that the agent observes but cannot modify; and 3) a research log of all iterations. They differ in adaptation, scale, and cost regime (Table [I](#S2.T1)). We introduce the two systems in the following paragraphs.\nII-A System A: Embedding Generation\nSystem A generates retrieval embeddings for 98% of the active catalog, translating to tens of millions of books. For the purpose of this study, we use Recall@6 as the objective metric: for each held-out book a customer purchased, the six nearest neighbors in embedding space are retrieved via a FAISS [[20](#bib.bib9)] IVF+Flat index, and Recall@6 measures how often these similarity-based recommendations include the customer’s actual next purchase. Top 6 is a representative approximation of visible items in the recommender’s interface. The input data comprises a co-purchase behavioral graph suitable for Node2Vec-style [[21](#bib.bib10)] training and pre-computed text embeddings from a sentence transformer [[22](#bib.bib11)]. The hand-tuned baseline against which the agent is compared is the product of four engineer-weeks of manual work: a Node2Vec implementation on the co-purchase graph, distributed training on GPUs, and iteration over loss functions and negative sampling, reaching 3.79% Recall@6.\nII-B System B: Configuration-Level Semantic ID Assignment\nSystem B learns hierarchical Semantic IDs [[6](#bib.bib6)] for over 50% of browsed books (in the order of millions) via RQ-VAE [[5](#bib.bib8), [4](#bib.bib7)], which encodes each book’s continuous embedding into a sequence of discrete codes, each level quantizing the residual left by\nthe previous one. Books that agree on their first code fall in the same coarse cluster; agreeing on the first two codes places them in a finer sub-cluster, and so on—each additional code is a deeper level of a browse-path-like hierarchy. Our primary metric, weighted coherence, measures how similar books within a cluster are. Writing \\(L1\\), \\(L2\\), \\(L3\\) for the mean cosine similarity between book embeddings within clusters at the first three levels, weighted coherence is \\(0.5\\times L1+0.3\\times L2+0.2\\times L3\\). The weights reflect customer-flow dominance: top-level prefix matches are the most frequent shared-prefix event across the catalog and most directly capture browsable cluster structure, so \\(L1\\) carries half the weight, while \\(L3\\) contributes the smallest share because deeper levels fragment quickly with cohort size. The weights are fixed across all System B runs and were not tuned to any individual configuration.\nWe also use secondary metrics approximating genre purity and cluster usability (median size, singleton rate, collision rate, etc.). The agent operates at the configuration and architecture level—proposing changes to hyperparameters and encoder architecture but not rewriting the training script. The hand-tuned baseline is a manually configured RQ-VAE trained on behavioral embeddings with hand-tuned codebook size, learning rate, and commitment weight (2–3 days of prototyping), reaching weighted coherence of 0.348—already well above the random-assignment baseline weighted coherence of 0.26.\nII-C Cost Profile and Campaign Scale\nThe combined campaign spans twelve weeks and 220+ experiments. System A ran 60+ iterations across 6 runs; System B ran 150+ iterations across 15 runs. A single System A iteration costs roughly 760\\(\\times\\) a System B iteration (combining instance hourly price, GPU count, and wall-clock duration); the two thus differ in per-iteration cost by nearly three orders of magnitude, making them an unusually informative pair for studying cost-dependent design choices.\n| Dimension | System A | System B | \n|---|---|---|\n| Search level | Code-level (train.py) | Config + arch. | \n| Iteration wall-clock | 9–19 hours | 6–52 min, 1 GPU | \n| Cost per iteration | \\(\\sim\\)760\\(\\times\\) System B | baseline | \n| # iterations | 60+ across 6 runs | 150+ across 15 runs | \n| Metric | Single scalar (R@6) | Multi-criteria | \n| Steering | Criticizer (automated) | program.md (human) | \n| Agents | 3 (Researcher, Fixer, Critic) | 1 (Researcher) | \nIII Failure Modes at Production Scale\nWe organize the failure modes by their root cause, distinguishing modes that follow from cost (A, D) from those that follow from long horizons (B, C) and those that follow from evaluation design (E). The first four modes were observed independently in both systems despite their different cost profiles; metric fixation (mode 5) requires multi-criteria evaluation and is therefore specific to System B, whose primary metric competes with secondary usability criteria (System A optimizes a single scalar and cannot exhibit it).\nIII-A Infrastructure Fragility\nIterations may fail from environmental reasons—evaluation timeouts, out-of-memory (OOM) crashes, GPU distribution bugs, malformed inter-agent messages—consuming budget without producing a usable signal. In System A, a GraphSAGE model exceeded the memory of multiple GPUs, and—before the Code Fixer was introduced—a multi-GPU wrapper bug ran 70M text encodings on 1 of the GPUs for over 10 hours. This class of fault is exactly what later motivated the pre-execution Code Fixer; once deployed, it caught recurrences of the same DDP misconfiguration before they reached hardware (Section IV-B). In System B, 11 of 30 iterations in a single run failed because the evaluator could not process a 10\\(\\times\\) batch size of semantic IDs within its 600-second timeout; eleven consecutive iterations were wasted before a human intervened. Coordination fragility is invisible in single-agent settings: it emerges only when multiple agents communicate through structured protocols and either end can silently corrupt the other’s input. Root cause: the agent has no model of infrastructure constraints, and agent-to-agent channels can degrade without either end recognizing the breakdown.\nIII-B Agent Memory Decay\nThe agent re-tests configurations that have already failed, sometimes repeatedly, rationalizing each re-test as “this time the context is different.” In System B, training for 100 epochs was tested and failed (due to overfitting) at least six times across multiple runs, despite program.md explicitly stating “100 epochs causes overfitting—do not re-test.” System A showed the same pattern at code level: in one run the Researcher re-introduced the same prohibited file-system operation six times across four iterations before any training completed, and across the campaign roughly five iterations were lost re-exploring configurations already recorded as failures. Root cause: a limited context window prevents holding full history; no persistent memory of negative results across runs; and the agent’s tendency to rationalize, consistent with broader observations of LLM confabulation [[23](#bib.bib14)]; external memory mechanisms such as MemGPT [[19](#bib.bib20)] reduce but do not eliminate this pattern.\nIII-C Search-Direction Stagnation\nThe agent reaches a local optimum and continues making marginal tweaks producing changes within noise, without recognizing the search direction is exhausted. In System A, without external redirection, the Researcher would have continued fusing pre-compressed 128d features indefinitely. In System B, one run spent five consecutive iterations attempting to improve a previous architecture, all within \\(\\pm\\)0.008 weighted coherence. Another tested commitment weight at four values producing results within 0.01 of each other. Root cause: the agent lacks a meta-level view of whether the current search direction has diminishing returns.\nIII-D Iteration-Cost Asymmetry\nThe agent selects experiments without weighing expected information gain against compute cost, treating a 15-hour multi-GPU run as no more consequential than a 30-minute ablation. This is distinct from infrastructure fragility: fragility describes why iterations fail; cost asymmetry describes how the agent decides what to run in the first place. In System A, the agent proposed a GraphSAGE variant requiring significantly more GPU memory than the baseline without flagging the compute overhead—the expected gain did not justify the marginal cost, but the agent had no mechanism to recognize this. In System B, a behavior-based auxiliary loss was explored across 10 consecutive iterations with negligible genre purity improvement before the run was terminated; each iteration was cheap, but the accumulated cost of an unproductive search direction was not. A separate failure—catastrophic codebook collapse dropping weighted coherence from 0.562 to 0.503 from a single parameter change—illustrates a related asymmetry: the agent assigned equal prior probability to high-consequence and low-consequence changes, with no mechanism to hedge against irreversible moves. Root cause: the agent has no model of information gain relative to compute expenditure, and no prior over the consequence severity of proposed changes.\nIII-E Metric Fixation\nThe agent optimizes the given metric faithfully but never questions whether it is the right metric, whether targets are feasible, whether the tradeoffs to meet the metric are reasonable, or whether the evaluation design has flaws. In System B, the agent discovered that maximizing codebook size trivially improves weighted coherence by creating more fine-grained clusters, but the resulting hierarchy was unusable—deep levels collapsed into singletons. We had to introduce a guardrail constraining n_levels and add a tertiary cluster-usability metric to prevent the agent from gaming coherence at the expense of a meaningful hierarchy. More strikingly, even with these additions, the three usability criteria (weighted coherence, genre purity, and median cluster size) were never simultaneously met in any of 15 runs across 150+ iterations, indicating that the tradeoff was structural rather than a tuning problem. While System A did not observe metric fixation as a failure due to it having a single optimization target, production systems will often require more complex, multi-objective targets. Root cause: the agent treats the metric as ground truth, not as a design choice.\n| Failure mode | System A (60+ iter) | System B (150+ iter) | \n|---|---|---|\n| Infra. fragility | 18% iter | 37% (worst run) | \n| Memory decay | \\({\\sim}\\)5 inc. | \\({\\geq}6\\) inc. | \n| Stagnation | 5+ iter | 5+ iter | \n| Cost asymmetry | Cost wasted per crash | 10 iter wasted | \n| Metric fixation | N/A | 0/15 runs met | \nThe five modes share a common origin: small-scale experiments have cheap iterations, simple metrics, short sessions that fit in context, and negligible failure costs. Each principle addresses a specific subset of the failure modes from Section III: prevention targets infrastructure fragility and iteration-cost asymmetry; redirection targets search-direction stagnation; persistence targets memory decay. Metric fixation is only partially mitigated, as we discuss at the end of this section. Table [III](#S4.T3) summarizes how each principle is instantiated in the two systems. The principles are not specific to this application: any long-horizon agent loop with asymmetric failure costs, context resets between sessions, and a search space with local optima faces the same structural pressures. The model split is intentional: Sonnet for the high-volume code-generation path; Opus for the lower-frequency, higher-leverage roles where a wrong call wastes hours of multi-GPU compute. It produces a complete, self-contained training script with full freedom in architecture, loss function, and optimization strategy.\nIV-B Prevention: Code Fixer\nThe Code Fixer reviews each Researcher-produced script before execution, catching semantic bugs that syntactic validation cannot detect: GPU underutilization, distributed-training misconfigurations, unnecessary recomputation, and memory leaks. Critically, the Code Fixer rewrites the proposed code in a single pass rather than describing bugs in natural language for a separate authoring step. The single-pass design was a response to an earlier two-step Reviewer-based architecture that we abandoned: across one campaign, the Reviewer returned malformed JSON on 14 consecutive calls, and the Researcher re-introduced the same prohibited file-system operation six times across four iterations before any training completed. The pattern is consistent across both systems: the agent optimizes effectively within a paradigm, but paradigm shifts require external redirection—automated for cheap-to-emit directives, human for cross-run programs.\nIV-D Persistence: Cross-Job Memory\nWe extend Karpathy’s program.md mechanism in two directions. First, we move it to durable cross-job storage so each new training job inherits the accumulated discoveries of all previous jobs (System A: 6 runs spanning \\({\\sim}\\)10 weeks). Second, in System B the human researcher (rather than the agent) maintains the program file between runs, encoding constraints, revert thresholds, and known findings the agent should not re-discover. The program’s effectiveness is partial: the agent re-tested 100-epoch training at least six times across multiple runs despite the program explicitly prohibiting it. The mechanism reduces the frequency of memory decay but does not eliminate it.\nSystem B also adds automatic revert, which triggered on 4 of 21 iterations in one run when the agent explored configurations that caused OOM failures. Multi-metric reporting expanding beyond the primary metric allowed the agent to observe and incorporate secondary signals, although a single optimization target remained; usability context in program.md provided explanations of why each metric mattered downstream; and human QA between runs diagnosed metric fixation early on and mitigated with competing criteria. These helped the human researcher minimize metric-gaming, but the fundamental problem remained that the agent itself never questioned the metric. While this is by design (the agent can only modify training parameters, not the evaluation criteria), it makes it impossible for the system to run completely hands-free. Whether this is solvable within current LLM capabilities is, in our view, an open problem (Section VI).\nV Results\nV-A System A: Recall@6 Trajectory\nThe hand-tuned baseline was the product of four engineer-weeks of manual work. Configuration-level AutoResearch—the same paradigm but with the agent restricted to hyperparameter and architecture edits—plateaued around baseline, unable to surpass the manually tuned result. Code-level AutoResearch with the full three-agent framework reached a 1.82\\(\\times\\) relative lift over the hand-tuned baseline (3.79%) and 2.45\\(\\times\\) over the deployed retrieval baseline (2.82%) on its peak evaluation date (\\(\\pm\\) 0.28 pp across 11 evaluation dates; the peak is \\({\\sim}\\)2.6\\(\\sigma\\) above the across-date mean), an iter-12 result obtained over six AutoResearch runs spanning \\({\\sim}\\)10 weeks, within the broader twelve-week campaign that also includes the manual-engineering phase shown in Figure [2](#S4.F2). The agent additionally produced a 5.8\\(\\times\\) catalog-coverage scale-up via an autonomously designed text-only fallback, which we describe separately in Section V-B. Total compute: 60+ iterations. We report this as an offline study; an online pilot of the embeddings is underway (Section VI).\nComponent contribution. A fully randomized A/B over the multi-week\ncampaign was not feasible; the natural campaign transitions in Table [V](#S5.T5) serve as the ablation. Three lines of evidence support a\ncausal rather than coincidental reading. Second, the Criticizer’s largest impact is sequentially documented: the “input representation problem” directive (Run 6, iteration 4) precedes the largest improvement attributable to a single directive (+50 bps over the next three iterations) with no intervening architectural change; the improvement is a spike, not a drift. Pre-Critic best.\nV-B System A: Autonomous Coverage Scale-Up\nThe trajectory in Figure [2](#S4.F2) reports Recall@6 on the held-out evaluation set, but it understates a second result: midway through the campaign, the agent independently identified and solved a catalog-coverage gap that the hand-tuned baseline had left open. The behavioral co-purchase graph covers only \\({\\sim}\\)17% of items—only the items with at least one observed co-purchase edge—leaving \\({\\sim}\\)83% of items in the broader catalog without any embedding. The hand-tuned baseline simply omitted these items from the FAISS index, capping retrieval at \\({\\sim}\\)17%.\nIn an iteration whose primary objective was an unrelated loss-function experiment, the Researcher noticed the coverage gap and proposed a text-only fallback: items absent from the behavioral graph would receive an embedding inferred from their text features alone, projected into the same 128d space as the behavioral embeddings via a small MLP trained jointly with the main model. The Code Fixer’s pre-execution review caught one DDP wrapper bug in the fallback path; the iteration ran successfully and the next FAISS index covered all items. Coverage thus expanded by 5.8\\(\\times\\) in a single iteration, without any human direction. No human had specified the gap as a target; no program.md entry mentioned coverage.\nThis is the cleanest example in our data of AutoResearch operating beyond hyperparameter search. Several practitioner implications follow. First, scope expansion is qualitatively different from metric optimization: the agent did not improve Recall@6 (it adjusted; coverage and recall trade against each other), but it removed a structural limitation of the system. Second, autonomous scope expansion is only useful when the agent has access to the metadata that exposes the gap—in our case, the coverage signal was available because the program included data-pipeline statistics. Without that signal, the agent would have continued optimizing on the \\({\\sim}\\)17% subset indefinitely. Third, the Code Fixer is essential here: the fallback path introduced a non-trivial DDP wrapper change, the kind of latent infrastructure fault that would otherwise have wasted the iteration entirely.\nV-C System B: Phased Progression\nSystem B exhibits the same redirection pattern as System A, but with the human—not an automated Criticizer—issuing the cross-run pivot.\n| Phase | Trigger / Focus | Best WC | Lift | \n|---|---|---|---|\n| Baseline | Hand-tuned (2–3 days) | 0.348 | 1.00\\(\\times\\) | \n| Phase 1 | Agent: architecture discovery | 0.691 | 1.98\\(\\times\\) | \n| Phase 2 | Human: scale to 10M + genre purity | 0.692 | 1.99\\(\\times\\) | \n| Phase 3 | Human: content-only embedding pivot | 0.633 | 1.82\\(\\times\\) | \n| Phase 4 | Human: behavioral+content pivot | 0.735 | 2.11\\(\\times\\) | \nTable [VI](#S5.T6) and Figure [3](#S4.F3) show the per-phase progression. This is the strongest evidence in our data that the failure taxonomy is structural rather than artifactual. The systems also share a common breakthrough pattern: the agent reaches a local optimum by incremental search, then external redirection (automated in System A, human in System B) reframes the problem and unlocks the next regime.\nV-E Validation Protocol\nWe took three precautions to confirm the reported gains are real rather than artifacts. First, robustness across evaluation dates. A single lucky evaluation date could flatter the result, so we re-evaluated System A’s iter-12 model on 11 distinct customer-purchase dates; the gain held throughout (peak 6.90%, mean 6.18% \\(\\pm\\) 0.28 pp, with the peak \\({\\sim}\\)2.6\\(\\sigma\\) above the across-date mean). Second, an immutable evaluator. In both systems the agent can read the evaluator’s output but cannot change its code, so it cannot inflate its own score by gaming the metric. Third, full-scale re-testing (System B). To rule out that the gains were an artifact of cheap, small-scale exploration, each architectural decision found in early runs (bottleneck encoder, deeper encoder, batch-size and codebook scaling) was re-tested at the larger Phase 2 data scale before being locked in for later phases.\nVI Discussion\nWhere AutoResearch delivers value. Despite the failure modes, AutoResearch delivered concrete value: throughput (150+ experiments in three weeks for System B; autonomous overnight runs for System A); systematic coverage that proved the coherence-density tradeoff was fundamental rather than a tuning problem; correct abandonment of dead ends; transfer of findings across runs; and autonomous problem-solving (System A’s 5.8\\(\\times\\) coverage scale-up; Section V-B). We estimate AutoResearch saved approximately 8 engineer-weeks across both systems. This estimate sums the engineer-equivalent of wasted System A iterations (18% of 60+ at 9–19 hours each), memory-decay re-tests across both systems, and the manual exploration cost that 220+ agent-driven experiments would have demanded at standard ML research rates.\nIteration cost as a design parameter. The two systems differ in per-iteration cost by orders of magnitude, and the scaffolding that made AutoResearch viable differed accordingly. The high-cost system benefits from a pre-execution Code Fixer that catches multi-hour bugs before resource allocation. The low-cost system relies on post-execution automatic revert that lets the agent attempt cheap hypotheses freely and rolls back on regression. This is a two-data-point empirical observation rather than a proven design principle, but we offer it as a parameter to consider when extending AutoResearch to other production deployments: when iterations are expensive, prevent; when iterations are cheap, react.\nThe human role at production scale. Human involvement shifts from running experiments to designing evaluations, writing research programs, and making strategic pivots—higher-leverage decisions while the agent handles systematic execution. More fundamentally, AutoResearch frees the human to move upstream: while the agent optimizes model parameters, the human focuses on whether the input data is right, whether the evaluation captures what matters, and whether the solution should be inside the model at all. System B exemplifies this: the agent’s 150+ iterations established the coherence–genre-purity tradeoff was structural, freeing the human to design a post-processing solution the agent could never have reached.\nPractitioner takeaways. For teams considering AutoResearch deployment, we offer five concrete recommendations distilled from the campaign:\n- 1. \nIdentify the smallest self-contained experimental loop (hypothesis \\(\\to\\) run \\(\\to\\) evaluation) that still produces meaningful signal. AutoResearch amplifies whatever loop it is given; over-broad loops waste budget, over-narrow loops cannot reach interesting improvements.\n- 2. \nFor iterations with non-negligible cost, add a pre-execution semantic gate (Code Fixer). The amortized savings of catching one DDP wrapper bug pay for the gate.\n- 3. \nPersist findings to durable cross-job storage from day one. program.md is not just a Karpathy artifact—it is the only mechanism that survives context-window resets.\n- 4. \nAdd a stagnation-only redirection signal. A Criticizer that fires every iteration produces noise; one that fires only on plateau produces paradigm shifts.\n- 5. \nProactively treat metric fixation as an unsolved problem. Design multi-metric reporting, encode usability context in the program, and budget human QA between runs. Do not expect the agent to question the metric.\nLimitations. Three limitations bound the scope of our\nconclusions. First, the component ablation for System A is\nobservational: the campaign transitions in Table [V](#S5.T5)\nare natural rather than controlled, and we cannot fully rule out\nthat accumulated iterations—rather than specific agent\ninterventions—contributed to the trajectory. While we address this through sequential and artifact-level evidence (Section V-A), a\ncontrolled replication on a different system would strengthen the\ncausal claim even further. Second, both systems operate within a single domain (book recommendation) at a single organization; whether the five failure modes and the cost-dependence principle generalize to other domains remains to be established. We discuss how the three principles map to other agentic domains under Beyond AutoResearch below, but empirical validation outside recommendation is future work. Third, the evaluation metrics (Recall@6, weighted coherence) are offline proxies that were chosen for the evaluation of these specific systems; neither has been validated against actual customer outcomes in this study, and the gap between offline improvement and production impact is a known risk in recommendation systems. Both metrics were chosen for their established correlation with the online metrics used in this pipeline, and online evaluation through the production A/B testing framework is the standard deployment path in our setting: online pilots of the System A embeddings and the System B Semantic IDs are underway, and validating the offline-to-online translation is the immediate next step for this work.\nOpen problems. Four problems remain open and motivate future work: cost-aware iteration planning—the agent currently weighs no information-gain-vs-compute tradeoff; parallel exploration across iterations to hedge multi-hour bets; cross-system agent communication (System A’s embeddings feed System B’s input, but the agents are disconnected); and metric self-evaluation, the most fundamental open problem—teaching agents to question whether the metric is right requires a form of meta-reasoning that current LLMs do not reliably perform.\nBeyond AutoResearch. The three principles described here—prevent, persist, and redirect—are not specific to ML research automation. They address structural properties that arise in any long-horizon autonomous agent loop: actions with asymmetric failure cost (motivating a pre-execution gate), context resets between sessions (motivating durable external memory), and search spaces with local optima (motivating stagnation-triggered reframing). The same triad applies to agentic software engineering, where a pre-execution semantic gate catches destructive migrations before CI/CD commits them, a cross-session memory preserves architectural decisions across coding sessions, and a stagnation signal redirects an agent cycling on a failing test rather than reconsidering the root cause. It applies equally to automated scientific experimentation, where preventing a failed wet-lab run is orders of magnitude more valuable than reverting it, accumulated findings must survive personnel turnover, and null-result patterns need to trigger hypothesis-level pivots rather than parameter-level adjustments. The cost-dependence insight transfers directly: the weight placed on each principle should scale with the irreversibility of actions, the length of sessions, and the dimensionality of the search space—not applied uniformly across all deployments.\nVII Conclusion\nThe most important lesson from twelve weeks of production-scale AutoResearch is about the agent’s boundaries. Both systems exhibit the same collaboration pattern: the agent exhaustively maps within-model limits, and the human designs around them. In System A, the Criticizer’s directive—“build explicit neighborhood representations”—drove the largest improvement we attribute to a single directive, a paradigm shift the Researcher could not reach through incremental code search. In System B, 150+ iterations established that the coherence–genre-purity tradeoff was structural, freeing the human to design a deterministic post-processing solution the agent could never have proposed. The three-agent framework—pre-execution prevention, cross-job persistence, and stagnation-triggered redirection—is what makes this collaboration pattern viable at production scale, where the cost of an unguarded iteration is measured in hours and hundreds of dollars. AutoResearch does not replace the researcher. It replaces the researcher’s weekends—and frees them to work on the problems that matter most.\nReproducibility\nThe orchestrator design (Researcher / Code Fixer / Criticizer agents, stagnation thresholds, sliding-window context management) and the evaluator protocol are described in Section IV and Section V.
