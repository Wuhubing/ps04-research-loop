# Research Loop: Delegate the Experiment, Keep Responsibility for the Claim

As of: 2026-10-15  
Evidence window: September 16–October 15, 2026

The useful development in this window is a more inspectable account of bounded delegation. NanoProteinLM describes recipe search with human selection and final ablation; a solar-panel segmentation study specifies what an agent may edit; Agora preserves experimental ancestry; and ResearchFlow-Eval checks whether experiments actually establish their intended claims. Read together, these reports support a practical interpretation: delegate a defined experimental loop while retaining responsibility for deciding what counts as scientific success. They do not establish that autonomous discovery is solved. <!-- 01-c1 01-c2 01-c3 01-c5 01-c6 01-c7 -->

NanoProteinLM offers a concrete result with an important boundary. Its authors report long-range contact precision increasing from 28.2% to 36.6% P@L at the same 48.4B-token budget, starting from the ESMC recipe. Humans decided which recipes to scale up. That gate belongs in any description of the result: the evidence concerns a search process that includes human judgment, rather than an agent independently taking responsibility for the entire research project. The matched token budget makes the reported comparison informative within its stated setting; it does not establish a general cost advantage for students. [NanoProteinLM authors’ account](https://jojoml.github.io/blog/2026/nanoproteinlm-agenticls/). <!-- 01-c1 -->

The confirmation stage matters as much as the headline improvement. Only four of seven proposed changes survived NanoProteinLM’s final ablation. The authors’ finding supports a methodological lesson: retaining a change during search should nominate it for confirmation, not settle its scientific value. For a student project, we would therefore separate permission to explore from permission to declare an improvement established. A promising recipe should face a distinct confirmation stage before becoming the basis of the project’s conclusion. That recommendation is our interpretation of the reported ablation, not an additional measured result. [NanoProteinLM](https://jojoml.github.io/blog/2026/nanoproteinlm-agenticls/). <!-- 01-c2 01-c7 -->

The solar-segmentation study makes the delegation boundary especially explicit. Agents may edit the training program, including the loss, optimizer, schedule and training-time augmentation. They cannot edit the evaluator or train on validation or test images. Each campaign is capped at 24 logged experiments, including its baseline. These restrictions give a student researcher a concrete procedure to inspect: identify the editable code, protect the evaluation, and declare the search budget before allowing iterative changes. Our reading is that this specification is a useful contribution even without treating any retained recipe as generally superior. [Solar-segmentation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10491). <!-- 01-c3 01-c7 -->

Its limitations also constrain the conclusions. Every proposed change uses a single random seed; Loops A and C receive no test evaluation; and coding-model choice is confounded with GPU class. Consequently, the results cannot isolate which coding model is generally better or establish a broadly reliable training recipe. This is a distinction between a bounded demonstration and a transferable recommendation. Students adopting the procedure should preserve that distinction when writing their own results: evidence from a particular campaign should remain attached to that campaign’s conditions. [Solar-segmentation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10491). <!-- 01-c4 -->

Agora addresses a different requirement: retaining the ancestry of an experiment. Its September publication describes an older run, from April 26 to May 8; the new evidence in this window is the published account, not a newly conducted experiment. The system uses immutable Git commits and parent relationships to preserve how contributions build on earlier work. However, the authors explicitly state that they did not rerun the winning method. We therefore interpret executable ancestry as useful infrastructure for inspection and subsequent reproduction, not proof that performance has been independently reproduced. [Agora](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18094). <!-- 01-c5 -->

ResearchFlow-Eval brings the acceptance question into focus. The authors report that 14 of 90 end-to-end claims met their criteria, including only one empirical claim. They also describe how a system’s reported success can concern a narrower experiment than the required claim. These findings warrant checking the relationship between an execution record and the scientific statement it supposedly supports. But the evaluation itself has limits: it uses an LLM judge, and its rubric follows an anchor paper, potentially undervaluing scientifically valid alternatives. Its counts should not become a universal success rate for research agents. [ResearchFlow-Eval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18088). <!-- 01-c6 01-c7 -->

Together, the reports suggest an experiment contract for students: specify the permitted change, freeze the evaluator, retain the experimental record, and write the scientific acceptance criterion before the search begins. Treat search-time gains as candidates for confirmation, and audit the final claim against the evidence actually produced. This is our synthesis across different studies, not independent corroboration of a shared result; no independent replication of these results is supplied. The defensible unit of delegation is a bounded experiment whose outcome can be inspected. Successful execution alone should never stand in for establishing the intended claim. <!-- 01-c2 01-c3 01-c5 01-c6 01-c7 -->
