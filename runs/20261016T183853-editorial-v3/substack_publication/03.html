For students exploring research agents, the next useful investment is a small experiment whose result another person can inspect. Our recommendation is to delegate bounded instruction changes and experimental execution while retaining human control over grading, candidate approval and interpretation. The sources below support different parts of that approach: operational guidance, researcher practice, claim-level evaluation and resource accounting. Together, they suggest a protocol, not a validated guarantee of trustworthy automation.

AI Code King’s tutorial provides a useful starting distinction. Despite its “self-training” title, the procedure leaves the underlying model unchanged: the optimizer edits workflow instructions, while grading rules stay fixed. This is instruction optimization, not training model weights. For a student project, that separation makes the experimental question concrete: do revised instructions improve performance under the same evaluation rules? The tutorial supplies an operational demonstration, not inspectable evidence of measured improvement. (00:56–01:01, 04:30–04:35)

The proposed comparison then moves beyond the examples used during search. The tutorial recommends evaluating original and optimized instructions on excluded tasks, using fresh project copies and sessions. It also asks the human to inspect the winning instruction changes. If that review produces edits, the edited version becomes a new candidate requiring evaluation. Human cleanup is therefore an experimental intervention, not an exemption from testing. These are procedural recommendations; the ambiguous rerun passage remains excluded pending audio review. (10:49–10:58, 13:29–13:43)

ResearchFlow-Eval adds a reason to preserve evidence for individual claims. Its authors report that only 14 of 90 end-to-end claims met their criteria, with just one empirical claim met. Their LLM judge supplied evidence locations and rationales, and a domain researcher spot-checked its assessments. Our inference is that students should retain human review of AI judgments, especially when an agent evaluates its own output. This is a safeguard, not evidence that judge bias occurred. The study’s scope also matters: its rubric follows one anchor task and may undervalue valid alternative approaches. (ResearchFlow-Eval)

There is a productive tension between the tutorial’s held-out comparison and ResearchFlow-Eval’s evaluation policy. The latter permits optimization using test sessions, provided every access records its purpose, configuration and result; optimization-informed results cannot be presented as untouched estimates. These procedures answer different questions. Our recommendation is to distinguish evidence used to choose a candidate from evidence used to assess its transfer. If evaluation data influenced selection, preserve that fact in the report rather than relying on the label “test.” (ResearchFlow-Eval; tutorial, 11:35–11:47)

That selection history belongs alongside ordinary research documentation. In Campus Talks, guests recommend connecting data to provenance, sharing variable descriptions and executed analysis code, and allowing extra planning time. They qualify sharing as appropriate and ethical. Applied to agent experiments, our interpretation is to budget documentation alongside execution: retain the instructions evaluated, the code run and the evidence behind the final choice. The podcast offers researcher guidance, not a tested agent intervention; its automatic transcript remains provisional, and the guests’ report and journal roles carry professional interests. (Campus Talks: 16:14–16:20, 39:07–39:10, 41:08–41:18, 54:36–54:40)

Budgeting also requires resisting the assumption that simple orchestration means inexpensive execution. Malena’s authors model GLM 5.2 inference costs at $12.12 for Malena versus $1.95 for AiScientist, while acknowledging that actual cache-hit token counts were unavailable. Those figures are modeled inference costs, not complete measured expenditure. The report also contains an unresolved inconsistency: its broad claim that Malena matches or beats every tested pairing conflicts with table estimates of 26.7 for Malena and 27.1 for AiScientist under Kimi-K3. We therefore use it to motivate resource measurement, not to establish performance superiority or savings. Students should record their own inference, compute and human review costs. (Malena paper)

The resulting student protocol is deliberately modest: fix grading before search, preserve candidate and data-access histories, compare on excluded work, review judgments and instruction edits, and account for resources. Its value is that another researcher can examine both the result and how it was selected. Future reports become more useful when they add research tasks, link judgments to evidence and demonstrate held-out improvements. None alone guarantees replication, and these sources do not independently validate our combined protocol. They identify what to demand before expanding delegation.
