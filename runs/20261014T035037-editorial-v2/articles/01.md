# Research Loop: Research Loops Became Easier to Inspect

As of: 2026-10-14  
Reporting window: September 15–October 14, 2026 (UTC)

The useful development in this reporting window is a richer record of bounded experimentation: comparisons with matched resources, explicit trial limits, traceable experiment histories and human decisions about what deserves further testing. Our reading is that these reports make delegation more testable. They do not establish general autonomous discovery. For student researchers, the central question should be whether an entire experimental loop produces evidence worth trusting—not how elaborate its agent architecture looks. <!-- 01-c6 -->

Start with the baseline. In [the Malena study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40303), the authors compare harnesses under matched compute resources, time budgets and backbone models. With GLM 5.2, they report a medal rate of 62.5%, against 47.1% for the best external harness tested. That is a benchmark-specific result, not a general estimate of research productivity. The authors also acknowledge possible benchmark contamination, limited statistical power and potential differences in tuning effort between systems. Those qualifications belong beside the headline result. <!-- 01-c1 -->

Malena’s orchestration experiments sharpen the practical question. The authors report that the base iteration performs no significantly worse than the coordination interventions they tested. This does not establish that collaboration never helps. It supports a narrower recommendation: before adding agents, ask whether the addition improves the result under the same constraints. Architectural complexity should earn its place through comparison. <!-- 01-c6 -->

[NanoProteinLM](https://jojoml.github.io/blog/2026/nanoproteinlm-agenticls/) moves the discussion from harness design to scientific evaluation. Its authors report long-range contact precision rising from 28.2% to 36.6% at the same 48.4-billion-token training budget, with a human deciding which recipes to scale. The reported gain therefore belongs to a workflow that includes human selection. Equal final training tokens also do not establish equal total search costs; students should resist reading that comparison as a complete accounting of efficiency. <!-- 01-c2 -->

The blog describes contact precision as a proxy for downstream protein folding, and reports that recipes improving validation loss could still fall well short of the best contact-precision setting. That distinction matters: success on the search objective needs a separate argument connecting it to the scientific goal. <!-- 01-c2 --> Four of seven changes survived the final ablation study. Our interpretation is that selection and subsequent testing are substantive parts of the research contribution, rather than administrative steps after automation has finished. <!-- 01-c6 -->

The [solar-panel segmentation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10491) offers a concrete model for limiting delegated authority. The agent may edit the training program, including the loss, optimizer and augmentation, but cannot edit the evaluator or train on validation or test images. Each campaign is capped at 24 logged experiments, including the baseline. Crucially, the one-hour allowance applies to an individual training trial, not the whole campaign. These details define both what the agent may change and how much searching the reported result permits. <!-- 01-c4 -->

Traceability addresses a different problem. [Agora](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18094) records contributions as Git commits linked to prior work, preserving relationships among results, hypotheses and verification. Its recent publication describes a run from April 26 to May 8: the new contribution in this reporting window is the account, not newly conducted experiments. That distinction prevents publication timing from becoming a misleading story about the pace of progress. <!-- 01-c3 -->

An inspectable history, however, is not proof that an agent uses it reliably. The [production-scale study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30541) reports eleven consecutive wasted iterations before human intervention, and repeated testing of an unsuccessful 100-epoch configuration despite explicit instructions against it. Persistent instructions did not eliminate repeated failures. <!-- 01-c5 --> Read alongside Agora, this suggests a useful distinction: preserving evidence and getting the next decision to respect that evidence are separate requirements. Students should inspect whether failures change subsequent behavior, not merely whether failures appear in a log. <!-- 01-c3, 01-c5 -->

Together, these reports favor delegating a bounded search over editable training choices while retaining explicit validation and selection gates. For a student project, our recommendation is to begin with a simple baseline, protect the evaluator, specify the search budget, retain unsuccessful trials and test selected changes before accepting the conclusion. Judge the loop by the quality of those decisions and comparisons. <!-- 01-c4, 01-c5, 01-c6 -->

The evidence remains limited. These are author-run studies and a first-party research blog from distinct reporting teams, not independent replications of a shared result; no outside researcher assessment is supplied. The production study’s component additions are observational, while Agora explicitly says that discovery per unit of compute requires a matched comparison. The defensible conclusion is therefore about inspectable workflow demonstrations. A broader claim that autonomous agents increase research productivity still needs stronger comparative evidence. <!-- 01-c5, 01-c6 -->
